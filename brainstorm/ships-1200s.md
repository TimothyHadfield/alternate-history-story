# Ships in the 1200s, and southern Africa

*Exploring possibilities. Ship facts are from memory; the population numbers are from the sources at the bottom.*

Tim (2026-10-27): "I don't have a good idea on ship capabilities. It seems like that's important because getting resources from afar or trading needs ships that can cross oceans. How many civilizations had ships and how many did they have? Is it something that I would need or be able to get if I started in south africa? Also what's the populatoin of people that live there?"

## Who had ocean-capable ships around 1230
| Who | Ships | Range | How many (rough) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Song China** | **Junks**: the biggest ships on Earth; several masts, watertight compartments, a sternpost rudder, and the **magnetic compass** (in use since the 1100s). Some carried several hundred people | All of East and Southeast Asia, India | Thousands of merchant ships; a large navy |
| **Arab, Persian and Indian traders** | **Dhows**: planks sewn together with rope (no nails), triangular lateen sails | The whole Indian Ocean, riding the **monsoon winds**: India ↔ Arabia ↔ East Africa as far south as **Sofala** | Thousands |
| **Javanese and Malay** | **Jong**: large multi-deck ships | Southeast Asia, India; their ancestors settled **Madagascar** centuries earlier | Many |
| **Venice, Genoa, Pisa** | **Galleys** (oars and sail, for war and luxury cargo) and **round ships** (sail, for bulk) | Mostly the Mediterranean and Black Sea | Venice could build a fleet of ~200+ for the 1202 crusade |
| **Norse** | **Knarr** cargo ships, longships | North Atlantic: Norway ↔ Iceland ↔ **Greenland** (the colony still exists) | Few, and declining |
| **Hanseatic towns** (Germany, Baltic) | **Cogs**: tall, flat-bottomed cargo ships | Baltic and North Sea | Many, and growing |
| **Polynesians** | **Double-hulled voyaging canoes** | Thousands of km across the open Pacific; reaching **New Zealand** about 1250–1300 | Small fleets |
| **Mongols** | Had none; later **borrowed** Chinese and Korean fleets to invade Japan (~900 ships in 1274, ~4,000 in 1281) | — | — |
| **Americas** | Canoes; the Chimú used **sailing balsa rafts** along the Pacific coast | Coastal | — |
| **Southern Africa** | **No ocean ships.** Swahili towns used sewn coastal boats; the long-distance trade was carried by Arab and Indian dhows | — | — |

**The big picture:** in 1230 **no one crosses the open Atlantic regularly**, and **nobody sails around the southern tip of Africa**. Ocean shipping is dominated by **China and the Indian Ocean traders**. The ships that later carry Europe around the world (caravels and carracks) don't exist until the 1400s.

## Why southern Africa is cut off by sea (good for hiding)
- The monsoon winds carry dhows only down to about **Sofala** (Mozambique). South of that, the strong **Mozambique and Agulhas currents** and the storms make the route dangerous, so Indian Ocean traders didn't go further.
- On the Atlantic side, nobody comes south until the Portuguese in the late 1400s.
- So the highveld can **trade through Sofala without anyone sailing to it**. That's the "one gate" in [homeland.md](homeland.md).

## Would a southern African start need ships?
**Not at first.** The highveld has nearly everything on land: iron, coal, copper, tin, gold, salt, cattle, grain. The early trade gate (gold out, cloth and beads in) already runs through **Sofala on Arab and Swahili ships**, so his people don't need their own.

**Later, yes**, for:
- trading on its own terms instead of through middlemen,
- reaching resources the region lacks (horses, wheat varieties, rubber, spices),
- exploration, and protecting the coast.

## Could he build them there?
**Yes.** Nothing is missing except experience:

| Need | In southern Africa? |
|---|---|
| **Timber** | Yes: the **Knysna forests** (yellowwood, stinkwood) on the south coast are excellent shipbuilding wood, and were used for exactly that later |
| **Iron** for nails, anchors, fittings | Yes: plenty, and iron-working is already known |
| **Rope and sailcloth** | Yes: Mapungubwe already **spun cotton** (spindle whorls are found there); local fibers for rope |
| **Tar / pitch** for waterproofing | Yes, from trees |
| **Harbors** | **Delagoa Bay (Maputo)**, **Durban**, **Sofala** |
| **Shipwrights** | None locally. He could teach the design and **hire dhow builders at Sofala or Kilwa** |
| **Sailors** | The hardest part: a skilled crew takes **years at sea** to train |

**The problem is distance:** Mapungubwe is about **400–500 km inland** and the Limpopo River is barely navigable. A shipyard means a **coastal town connected by roads** (later rail).

**What he could build that nobody else has yet:** a design combining **Chinese ideas** (sternpost rudder, watertight compartments, compass) with **later European ones** (a strong frame-first hull, mixed square and triangular sails like a caravel or carrack, and finding latitude with a quadrant or astrolabe). A small ship like that could sail in **~5–10 years** once there are tools, a shipyard and trained crew. Big ocean fleets take decades.

## Population of the region
- **Mapungubwe's capital:** about **3,000–5,000** people at its peak in the mid-1200s, on the hill and terraces.
- **The whole kingdom:** about **9,000 people** in ~80 homesteads under one ruler (UNESCO's estimate).
- **All of southern Africa** (south of the Zambezi): no good published number turned up. **Reasoned guess: a few hundred thousand to about a million**, mostly Bantu-speaking farmers in the north and east, and Khoisan herders and hunter-gatherers in the dry west and south.

**What that means:** it's **small**. Britain had ~6–9 million when it industrialized. A southern African start would need **fast population growth and immigration** (see [population.md](population.md)), which is one reason a two-stage start that brings people in keeps coming up.
