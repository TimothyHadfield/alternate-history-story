# Will they accept him, and change?

*For choosing. Ratings are Claude's judgment; history is from memory.*

Tim (2026-10-28): "I think it's also important to think about how these people might accept me and change their way of life. Are some cultures more or less resistient to this?"

## What makes a society accept a stranger's changes
Culture matters less than these five things:
1. **Does it help them fast and visibly?** A child saved, a bigger harvest, fatter cattle. People adopt what obviously works.
2. **Do their own leaders back it?** In a society with one strong ruler, **winning the ruler changes everyone quickly**. Without one, change spreads slowly, but no single ruler can stop it either.
3. **Does it leave their identity alone?** Tools and crops spread easily. Changes to **religion, family, land ownership or who's in charge** meet the most resistance.
4. **Are strangers normal there?** Trading peoples are used to foreigners and new goods; isolated peoples may see him as a wonder or a threat.
5. **Can anyone rise?** Where status is fixed by birth (castes, warrior classes, guilds), new ways threaten the people at the top.

**History says almost every people adopts useful things fast.** The **Plains peoples** remade their whole way of life around the horse within a few generations. The **Māori** took up potatoes and iron within decades. **Japan** was mass-producing guns within ~30 years of first seeing one. The **Basotho** of southern Africa (1800s) quickly adopted horses, ploughs and reading. **Resistance is mostly about control and identity, not about new tools.**

## The top 10 locations, rated for acceptance (/10)
| # | Place | /10 | Why it helps | Why it resists |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Southern Africa (Mapungubwe)** | **6** | One sacred king and a small elite: **win the king, win the kingdom**. Cattle are wealth, so anything that helps cattle and crops is welcome. Already trades for foreign beads and cloth. Later southern Africans adopted ploughs, horses and literacy fast | Language barrier, no writing. The king's power is tied to **rainmaking**, so a stranger with better answers could threaten the king and ritual experts unless he works *for* them (wells and irrigation can be the king's gift) |
| 2 | **Java** | **6** | The most **cosmopolitan** place on the list. Took in Hinduism, Buddhism and later Islam peacefully through traders and courts; strangers are normal | Hierarchical courts. Because foreigners are common, he's less special, and rivals and merchants will copy him |
| 3 | **Japan** | **5** | Once the elite decides, Japan **adopts faster than anyone** (Chinese writing, Buddhism, guns, the Meiji era) | Wary of outsiders. Status fixed by birth; a foreigner can't easily rise among the warrior class |
| 4 | **Britain** | **4** | Scholars were hungry for new knowledge (translating Arabic science); towns valued profit | Lords, guilds and religious authorities all guard their position. **Guilds resist new methods**, and a stranger has no standing |
| 5 | **Andes** | **5** | Communities (ayllu) already work together on big projects such as terraces and canals: a good fit for organized labor | Deeply tradition-bound farming and religion; no writing; many separate peoples in 1230 (the Inca empire comes later) |
| 6 | **North America** | **6** | Peoples who later adopted horses, metal and new crops very quickly. The biggest tech gap, so the benefits are obvious | **No single ruler to win**: many separate peoples and languages. Changing land use is a big identity question |
| 7 | **Ethiopia** | **4** | A strong central monarchy that loved great building projects (Lalibela's churches) | Very strong **Orthodox Christian identity** and suspicion of outsiders; the Church is powerful |
| 8 | **Sweden** | **5** | Free farmers, less rigid lords, practical Norse culture | Small, conservative farming communities; a foreigner is an outsider for a long time |
| 9 | **Southeast Australia** | **2** | Fast learners of useful tools | **The biggest change of all:** from hunting and gathering to farming and industry. Deep ties to land and law (the Dreaming); small separate bands. History here is mostly resistance and tragedy |
| 10 | **Central Chile (Mapuche)** | **3** | Took up horses fast (and used them against Spain) | **Fiercely independent:** resisted both the Inca and Spain for 300+ years; no central ruler to win |

**Refugees or settlers who choose to follow him (the two-stage idea): 9/10.** They chose him, already depend on him, and have no old order to defend. The catch: their old culture comes with them, and they must then live alongside the local people.

## How he could win acceptance anywhere
- **Start with what they already value:** cattle and rain in southern Africa, rice and trade in Java, swords and honor in Japan.
- **Work through their leaders**, so changes arrive as the leader's gifts, not the stranger's orders.
- **Leave religion and family alone at first.** Change tools, health and farming first; the rest follows slowly.
- **Prove things over and over in public**, and teach so anyone can learn (see [rise-to-power.md](rise-to-power.md)).
- **Let them own it:** local names, local people in charge of the new things.
- **Schools are the long lever:** children grow up with the new ways as normal.
