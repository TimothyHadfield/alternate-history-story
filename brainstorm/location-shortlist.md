**How "practical" is scored:** what a 1230s society can actually use: **draft animals and livestock, iron ore it can smelt, coal near the surface, timber, farmland and water, copper and tin, and transport (rivers, coast)**. Deep or remote deposits count for little. **Gold, silver and spices are scored separately** and weighted low: they only buy things from outsiders (horses, seeds, books, skilled people), which matters early but can't build industry.

**Overall weighting:** practical resources ×3, population and settlers ×2, acceptance ×2 (from [culture-acceptance.md](culture-acceptance.md)), safety ×2, trade valuables ×1.

**What changed:** valuing *usable* resources lifts **Britain** and **North America** and lowers **Java** (whose best assets are spices and trade). **Southern Africa stays on top, but only just**, because its most famous riches (deep Witwatersrand gold, diamonds, platinum) were out of reach in 1230.

### Practical resources, place by place
- **Southern Africa (7):** **cattle** in large numbers (oxen for ploughs and carts, and cowpox); **iron** ore everywhere and already smelted; **copper** at Musina (~70 km from Mapungubwe) and Phalaborwa, worked for centuries; **tin** at Rooiberg; **coal** in the Limpopo and Waterberg fields, roughly 50–250 km away, some near the surface. *Weak:* **no horses** (horse sickness and tsetse), droughts, little big timber on the plateau, **no navigable rivers**. *Not useful in 1230:* most Witwatersrand gold (deep, low-grade, needs 1880s chemistry), diamonds, platinum.
- **Britain (10):** **coal already being dug** where it outcrops (Newcastle "sea coal" in the 1200s), iron ore near it, horses, oxen, sheep, timber, rivers and coast everywhere, tin in Cornwall. Everything practical, close together.
- **North America (7 alone, 9 with brought animals):** the most accessible minerals anywhere: **coal in riverbanks** at Pittsburgh, iron ore, **pure copper lying at the surface** around Lake Superior, endless timber, the best farmland, and **navigable rivers everywhere**. But **no horses, oxen, cattle or wheat**, and no iron-smelting tradition.
- **Japan (7):** horses and cattle, excellent timber, **iron sand** (workable but labor-hungry), copper, **coal in Kyushu** near the surface and coast. Limited flat farmland.
- **Java + Australia (6):** Java has superb rice land, water, buffalo and **teak** (great ship timber). Nearby Bangka has easy alluvial **tin**. But little iron or coal. **Australia's surface iron (Pilbara) is enormous but 1,500+ km away in desert.**
- **Sweden (7):** Bergslagen **iron** (rich, already mined), **Falun copper** (open pit), huge timber, water power on every river, horses and cattle. **No coal**, but charcoal made Swedish iron the world's best for centuries. Poor farmland.
- **Andes (5):** **copper and tin** easy to reach, **guano**, the best fertilizer on Earth, on the coast, terraces and potatoes. But **llamas only** (no ploughing animals), little coal, iron ore in the coastal desert, brutal transport.
- **Central Chile (6):** **coal on the coast** (Lota), surface copper, good Mediterranean farmland, southern timber. Llamas only; remote.
- **Ethiopia (5):** **oxen and ploughs already in use**, horses, mules, highland wheat and barley. Little iron, no coal, no timber to speak of, cut off from the sea.
- **Southeast Australia (6):** **coal lying on the beach** at Newcastle (found that way in 1791), hardwood timber, harbors. No farm animals and no native crops to farm; droughts.

**Absorbing many newcomers at once** works best when they share a language family, a religion or a way of making a living with the locals. It also needs **food surpluses first**, since newcomers who arrive to famine cause conflict. And they should be **spread through existing villages** rather than packed into their own enclaves. ### One line on each
1. **Southern Africa highveld:** possibly the most mineral-rich land on Earth (gold, coal, iron, copper, tin, platinum), an existing kingdom with cattle and iron-working, and a hidden trade gate at Sofala. Weakness: **small population** (~9,000 in the kingdom).
2. **Java:** millions of skilled rice farmers and the hub of the spice trade. Weakness: **exposed** (China and India trade there; a Mongol fleet arrives in 1293) and short on coal and iron.
3. **Japan:** a skilled, organized people (~6–7 million), great smiths, an island defense. Weaknesses: **samurai society shuts out outsiders**, and its coal and iron are thin.
4. **Britain:** the place industry actually began, with coal and iron side by side and the sea at the door. Weakness: **crowded with kings, nobles and the Church**; very hard to reach the top.
5. **Andes:** silver, tin, copper, the world's best fertilizer (guano), and organized, hard-working people. Weaknesses: no strong draft animals, mountains make transport brutal, little coal.
6. **North America:** the best resources and farmland on Earth, with total isolation. Weakness: **no iron, horses, oxen or wheat, and few farmers**, so it works best if people and animals are brought in.
7. **Ethiopia:** a literate Christian mountain kingdom with cattle and grain, naturally defended. Weakness: few minerals and no coal.
8. **Sweden:** Europe's best iron ore and its biggest copper mine (Falun), plenty of timber. Weaknesses: no coal, a cold short growing season.
9. **Southeast Australia:** huge coal (Hunter Valley) and iron, the safest place in the world. Weakness: **no farmers or farm animals at all**, so everyone and everything must be brought in.
10. **Central Chile:** copper, coal near the coast (Lota), nitrates to the north, a Mediterranean climate, the Andes as a wall. Weakness: few people, very remote.

## Should there be a second site?
**Yes, but as an outpost that grows, not a second capital from day one.**

**What a second site gives:**
- **Insurance:** a hidden fallback if the homeland is found, invaded or hit by drought or plague.
- **What the homeland lacks:** people, crops, a different climate, a missing resource.
- **A trade gate kept away from home:** outsiders see the outpost and never the heartland.
- **Room to grow** once the first land fills up.

**What it costs:**
- It **splits a small population**.
- It **needs ocean ships early**, which take 5–10 years to build and longer to crew (see [ships-1200s.md](ships-1200s.md)).
- Messages take months, there are two places to defend, and a far-off outpost may break away.

**The rule of thumb:** a second site is worth it from day one **only if it can be reached by land or a short sail**. Far ones come later, after ~20–40 years, once ships and sailors exist.

## The strongest pair overall
**Southern Africa highveld, with the Cape coast first and Australia later.**
- **The Cape and Knysna coast** can be **reached overland**. It adds a **wheat-and-grape climate**, the **Knysna shipbuilding forests**, and good harbors, with no long sea trip.
- **Australia later:** from the Cape, the steady west winds of the **"Roaring Forties"** carry ships to Western Australia fast. Dutch ships used this route in the 1600s and made the trip in about two months. That gives a **hidden fallback** with the world's biggest iron deposits (Pilbara), and later **southeast Australia's coal**.
- **Madagascar** is close by sea if more rice and people are needed.

This keeps the **resources, safety and hidden trade gate** of #1. The far second site waits until ships exist.

**Claude's recommendation:** **#1, the southern Africa highveld with the Cape second, and Australia as the long-term fallback.** The runner-up is **#2, Java with Australia**, if Tim would rather start with millions of people and accept the exposure.
