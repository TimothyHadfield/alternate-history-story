# Britain: the Merchant Inside

*He never wears a crown. He builds a company, a school and a string of towns inside Henry III's England, and the money, the know-how and the coal smoke slowly carry the whole country into an early industrial revolution.*

*This is one variation, written for comparison with the other five. It is not a decision.*

## What makes this one different
- **No throne, no founding, no secret co-ruler.** In this variation he never rules anything bigger than his own company. His power is **economic and institutional**: money, contracts, charters, schools, towns. He is useful to every king and owned by none.
- **The group is two things at once:** a **company and its network of chartered towns**, which he controls, and **England itself**, which he can only nudge. The company grows fast; England grows with it.
- **He develops inside a crowded, competitive Europe.** France and Flanders are a day or two away by sea, so **anything he makes gets copied within years**. The other five variations hide their secrets. This one has to **out-run** its copiers instead.
- **It's the only variation that sits on the real birthplace of industry.** Coal on the Tyne, iron in the Weald and the Forest of Dean, tin in Cornwall, water power on every river, and the sea at every door. It aims at a **coal-and-water industrial revolution about 500 years early**.
- **The big danger isn't invasion. It's politics:** a king who's always broke, barons who rebel, and lenders who get ruined when the crown can't pay. He gets through it by being **neutral, useful and hard to seize**.

## The setup
| | |
|---|---|
| **Arrival** | Spring **1233**, on the south bank of the Thames at **Southwark**, across London Bridge from the City |
| **His age** | About 21 |
| **What he has** | His clothes and his head: an EE student's math, physics, chemistry, a rough grasp of medicine, history and economics |
| **The king** | **Henry III**, 25, king since he was 9. He begins his **personal rule in 1234** after the fall of Peter des Roches. Pious, artistic, generous to foreign favorites, and **always short of money** |
| **The country** | About **4 million people** *(estimates range from 3.5 to 5 million)*, growing fast toward a peak around 1300. Villages, open fields, sheep everywhere. London has perhaps 40,000–80,000 people *(uncertain)* |
| **The economy** | **Wool** is England's oil: raw wool goes to the looms of **Flanders** and Italy. Wine comes in from Gascony, which the English king also rules |
| **Learning** | **Oxford** is one of Europe's leading universities. **Robert Grosseteste** lectures there to the Franciscans until 1235. Cambridge was founded in 1209 |

**Language is his first wall.** Three languages run England:
- **Middle English** for ordinary people. Modern English grew out of it, so in about **6–12 months** he can follow it and in 1–2 years speak it badly but usefully. Spelling and many words differ; the sounds differ more.
- **Anglo-Norman French** for the court, the barons and the law.
- **Latin** for the Church, the universities and records. He knows some from science words; he needs years to write it well.

A foreigner with an odd accent is normal in London. Italians, Flemings, Gascons, Germans and Jews all live and trade there. That's his cover: **just another strange merchant.**

## The rise to power
Power here means **being too useful to remove**, not ruling. Each step stacks a new kind of usefulness.

| Years | Step | What he does |
|---|---|---|
| **1233–35** | **Survive and earn** | Works as a laborer, then a **clerk for a London merchant** (a pepperer or wool man). His first edge is just arithmetic: **Arabic numerals and zero** (Fibonacci's book exists but almost nobody in England uses it) and **double-entry bookkeeping**, 260 years before Pacioli. A merchant who can always tell what he owns and owes is a merchant who doesn't get cheated |
| **1235–37** | **The first products** | Small, cheap, repeatable things he can sell himself: **distilled spirits** (aqua vitae for medicine and preserving), **hard soap**, and later **reading spectacles** (convex lenses ground from clear glass or rock crystal; in history they appear in Pisa around 1286) |
| **1236–38** | **Oxford and Grosseteste** | Grosseteste, now **Bishop of Lincoln (1235–53)**, wrote about lenses, light and rainbows. The hero brings him spectacles and an experiment with a prism. Grosseteste becomes his **patron, protector and the one man told the truth**, proved once with a sealed letter that names something only time can confirm. He never makes a second prophecy |
| **1238** | **The Company** | He forms a **partnership with shares**: London merchants put in money, he puts in methods, and everyone shares the profit. It's a joint-stock company 350 years before the East India Company. In this text it's just "the Company"; its real name is Tim's to choose |
| **1238–45** | **Charters** | Henry III sells charters to raise cash. The Company buys **royal letters patent** for its products (a monopoly in England for a set number of years) and later a **charter for its own town**. England had no patent law yet; the first known invention patent came in 1449. Here the Company asks for them from the start |
| **1240s** | **The king's accountant** | The Exchequer runs on **tally sticks and Roman numerals**. He offers clean accounts and simple forecasts. When Henry plans the **1247 recoinage** (the "Long Cross" penny, made to stop people clipping silver off coins), he suggests a **milled edge** (ridges on the rim), which makes clipping obvious. A tiny idea with huge value |
| **1250s** | **Banker without the trap** | Henry's "**Sicilian Business**" (1254) buries him in debt. The Company **refuses to become his main lender**. It sells him services instead: grain, accounts, ships, iron |
| **1258** | **The cold year** | He has quietly stored grain since 1255 and has Baltic grain contacts. When the harvest fails, **the Company feeds London**. Everyone owes him: king, barons and the city |
| **1264–65** | **Neutral in the civil war** | Simon de Montfort rules England after the Battle of Lewes; London backs him. The Company feeds both sides' towns, lends to neither, and keeps its works running |
| **1272–75** | **Edward I** | Edward is a king of statutes, accounts and merchants. In history he hands the new wool customs (1275) to the **Riccardi of Lucca**. Here **the Company collects them**. **This is when he holds real power**: the Company handles much of the crown's cash flow, employs tens of thousands, and its towns send burgesses to parliament |

**Key people to win:**
- **Robert Grosseteste** (the patron and the one he tells).
- **London's mayor and aldermen** (Andrew Bukerel is mayor from 1231 to 1237): his first partners.
- **Richard of Cornwall**, the king's brother, the richest man in England and a businessman with German contacts. He's the natural **investor**.
- **The Lord Edward** (born 1239). He's taught by Company men from boyhood if possible. The hero stays clear of factions but gets close to the heir.

**The risks, and how this variation handles them:**
- **The crown default.** The Riccardi were ruined in 1294 and the Bardi and Peruzzi in the 1340s, all because kings didn't pay. **Rule: never lend the crown more than the Company could lose.** Most of the Company's worth is in **skills, tools and towns**, which a king can't carry off.
- **The favorite's fall.** Henry's foreign favorites (the Savoyards, the Lusignans) made barons furious. **He takes no title, no castle and no royal bride.** He marries into a London merchant family instead.
- **Simon de Montfort.** He's brilliant, admired and dangerous to back. Montfort's parliament of January 1265 seats burgesses from the towns. The hero likes that idea and helps it survive Evesham in another form, without ever choosing a side on the battlefield.

## How the group grows
**The group** is the Company (its staff, families and towns) plus England. The Company grows by **pulling people in**; England grows by **not dying** (no famine deaths, far fewer child deaths, and later no Black Death collapse).

**Population sources:**
| Source | Why they come | Rough numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **Births** | Clean water, soap, midwife training, boiled milk and better food cut child deaths by maybe a third to a half | The biggest source over 100 years |
| **Runaway serfs** | An old custom: a villein who lives **a year and a day in a chartered town** is free. Every Company town is chartered | Thousands a year by the 1260s |
| **Flemish weavers** | England has the wool; Flanders has the skill. Better pay, and English cloth no longer needs Flanders | Hundreds of families, then thousands. Historically Edward III invited them in the 1330s |
| **German miners** | For the Cornish tin, the Tyne coal and the Weald iron. Historically they came in the 1500s | Hundreds |
| **Jewish families** | Edward I expels them in 1290 (about 2,000–3,000 people) | The Company offers them work as financiers, doctors and clerks, in its towns or its overseas offices |
| **Gascons, Welsh, Scots, Irish** | Wages | Steady |
| **Refugees** | Famine years across Europe (1258, 1315) | Waves |

**Target numbers** (Company network / all England, rough):
| Year | Company network | England |
|---|---|---|
| 1240 | 60 | 4.0M |
| 1260 | 8,000 | 4.6M |
| 1280 | 60,000 | 5.2M |
| 1300 | 250,000 | 6.0M (history: ~4.5–5M) |
| 1348 | 1M+ | 7–8M (history: falls to ~2.5–3M after the Black Death) |

**How food, housing and health keep up:**
- **Food:** clover in the crop rotation (more cattle, more manure, more grain), iron ploughs from Company furnaces, and Baltic grain imports in bad years. England already has the markets; he adds **storage and forward contracts**.
- **Housing:** brick and tile from Company kilns (fire-safe, unlike thatch), lime mortar, and **planned town grids with drains**, like Edward I's real new towns (Winchelsea, 1288).
- **Health:** see below. The rule is that **a town is chartered only with its water and sewers built in.**

## Government and society
**How it's run:** England stays a kingdom with a king, barons, the Church, shires and the common law. The hero changes it **from the inside**, through institutions that suit English habits:
- **The Company** is run like a guild crossed with a modern firm: shareholders, an elected **Court of Governors**, written rules, and **audited books read aloud once a year**.
- **Company towns** get **royal charters** like any borough: their own mayor, courts and market. Their charters also add **sanitation, schooling and fire rules**.
- **Parliament:** Montfort's 1265 parliament and Edward's 1295 "Model Parliament" brought in town burgesses. Company towns send theirs. Over decades, **merchants and makers gain a real voice** without anyone being overthrown.
- **Law:** Edward's **Statute of Merchants** (1283, 1285) made debts enforceable. The hero pushes for **contract law, patents and insurance** in the same style.

**Fitting the culture:** the English already value charters, juries, written custom and "ancient rights". He frames everything new **as a charter, a statute or a guild rule**, never as a revolution.

**Schools:**
- **A Company house at Oxford** (in history, Walter de Merton founds **Merton College in 1264**). The hero funds a house for **mathematics, natural philosophy and engineering**, with **Roger Bacon** (born about 1219/1220) as its star. Bacon wanted experiment-based science and wrote about lenses and gunpowder. Here he gets a workshop.
- **Petty schools** in every Company town: reading, writing, Arabic numerals and bookkeeping for every child. Clever children from anywhere get places.
- **Apprenticeships** with written manuals.

**Succession:** he plans for it from 1260, though he expects to live long.
- The Company is owned by **shares, not by his family**. His children hold shares; the Governors pick the leader.
- He writes **the Founder's Books**: methods, the order to build things in, and **sealed instructions for the events he knows are coming** (1315, 1348), to be opened by the Governors at set dates. No prophecy in public: just orders such as "build up grain stores from 1310" and "keep rats out of the warehouses."
- **Three trained successors at a time**, so no single death breaks it.

## The tech path
Why this order *for Britain*: **money and institutions first** (a stranger with no land needs income and protection), **then cloth and coal** (England's wool and the Tyne's sea-coal are already traded), **then iron and steam** (Weald and Dean iron, plus the need to pump water out of deep coal and tin mines), **then transport and power**. Rivals copy the products, so he builds an edge they can't copy quickly: **machine tools, trained people and coal**.

| Phase | Years | Key tasks and inventions | Why now, why here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0–2** | 1233–35 | Arabic numerals, **double-entry bookkeeping**, distillation, soap, **handwashing and boiling water**, a better balance scale | Costs nothing but knowledge. Earns trust and cash in London |
| **3–5** | 1236–38 | **Spectacles**, clear glass, **standard units** for the Company, simple **insurance** for cargo, the **joint-stock Company**, letters **patent** | Lenses win Grosseteste and every aging merchant and scribe. Institutions protect what comes next |
| **6–10** | 1239–43 | **Paper mill** (water-driven; London rags), **printing press** (movable type: the Company prints its own manuals, ledgers and contracts), the **spinning wheel** and a **multi-spindle spinner**, more **fulling mills**, crop rotation with **clover**, **iron ploughs and scythes** | England already has ~6,000 water mills. Paper and print make the schools and the Company scale. Cloth keeps English wool's value at home |
| **11–20** | 1244–53 | The first **coal works on the Tyne** (Newcastle's burgesses got a licence to dig coal around 1239), **wooden wagonways** from pit to river, **blast furnace** in the **Weald** (Sussex) and the **Forest of Dean** (iron ore, coal and timber beside the Severn, with Bristol as the port), **cast iron**, **lathes and boring mills**, **telescope**, **thermometer and barometer**, the **Oxford house** | Dean and the Weald already make iron (St Briavels in Dean made crossbow bolts for the crown). Machine tools are the "tools that make tools" and the secret rivals can't easily copy |
| **21–50** | 1254–83 | **Coke** from Tyne coal, the **coke blast furnace**, **Newcomen steam engine** pumping **Cornish tin mines** and deep Tyne pits, a **water-powered spinning and weaving mill**, **canals and locks**, **milled coinage**, **microscope**, **germ theory taught** in the Company schools, **cowpox vaccination**, **sulfuric acid, bleach, soda**, interchangeable parts | Mines flood as they go deeper. That need drove the real Newcomen engine (1712), and Cornwall and the Tyne were its first customers. Coal replaces scarce firewood around London |
| **51–100** | 1284–1333 | **Watt engine**, **iron rails and locomotives** on the Tyne and to London, **steamships** on the Thames and across the Channel, **steam cloth mills** in the wet Pennine valleys, **semaphore telegraph**, **public schooling** across England, **Portland cement**, **sewers and clean water** for London, **electricity** (batteries, electromagnets, the telegraph) by the end | By now the gap with rivals is machines and the people trained to run them. The Founder's Books hand the electrical work to his successors |

**Local resources and where they are:**
| Resource | Where |
|---|---|
| **Sea-coal** at the surface | **Newcastle upon Tyne** and the Durham coast, shipped to London by sea |
| **Iron ore + timber** | **The Weald** (Sussex and Kent) close to London; **Forest of Dean** near the Severn |
| **Tin** | **Cornwall and Devon**, already run by the royal **stannaries** (charter of 1201) |
| **Lead and silver** | The Pennines, the Mendips |
| **Wool** | Everywhere, the best from the Cotswolds and Lincolnshire |
| **Clay, lime, building stone** | Everywhere; Cotswold limestone; brick clay in the Thames valley |
| **Water power** | Every river; the fast Pennine streams for later mills |
| **Salt** | Cheshire brine springs (Droitwich and Northwich), sea salt on the coast |

## Food, water and health
**The first tasks, in order:**
1. **Boil water, wash hands, use soap.** In the Company's own house in year 1, then in every workshop. It costs nothing and cuts deaths from diarrhea.
2. **Latrines away from wells**, and the Company's water drawn upstream of its works.
3. **Clean childbirth.** Midwives trained to wash hands and tools in spirits; this is the single biggest lifesaver for women.
4. **Oral rehydration** (salt, sugar or honey, and boiled water) for children with diarrhea.
5. **Clover and turnips** in the rotation, for more cattle, milk and winter fodder.
6. **Grain stores** with stone floors and **cats and rat-proofing**, which is also practice for 1348.
7. **Quarantine and delousing** for ships at Company wharves.
8. **Cowpox vaccination** once he finds cowpox among dairy cows (it's common in English cattle: Jenner's discovery came from Gloucestershire milkmaids).

**Getting ready for the Black Death (1348).** He'll likely be dead by then, so it's written into the Company's charters and the Founder's Books decades earlier:
- **Germ theory** taught in every Company school from the 1270s, shown under a microscope.
- **Rat and flea control:** stone and brick warehouses, sealed granaries, cats, rat-catchers paid per tail, and flea-killing fumigation of cargo and clothes.
- **Quarantine law:** 40 days for ships from infected ports (Venice and Ragusa invented this in the 1370s; here it comes decades earlier).
- **Plague hospitals** outside towns, nurses with masks and washed gowns, and burying or burning the dead's bedding.
- A **news network** (the Company's agents in Genoa, Bordeaux and Calais) to warn months ahead.

The plague struck in 1348, entering through Melcombe in Dorset. Prepared towns may lose **5–15% instead of 40%**. That number is a guess, not a measurement.

## Trade and the outside world
**What goes out:** **finished cloth** (instead of raw wool), cast and wrought iron, tools, tin and pewter, **coal to Flanders and France**, glass, spectacles, paper, printed books, soap, spirits, and later steam engines and machines.

**What comes in:** wine from Gascony, **Baltic grain, timber, tar and furs**, Spanish and Swedish iron (until Company iron is better), alum from the Mediterranean (for dyeing), dyes and spices through the Italians, and **skilled people** from everywhere.

**The gate:** **London**, with the **Company's own wharves** at Southwark. Later there are second gates at **Bristol** (Severn and Dean) and **Newcastle** (coal), with Company offices in **Bruges**, **Bordeaux** and **Lübeck**.

**The rivals-copy-everything problem.** France and Flanders are a day or two away, and Italian merchants live in London. A secret here lasts **years, not generations**. This variation doesn't try to hide everything. It sorts its knowledge into three bins:

| Bin | Examples | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Give away** | Soap, handwashing, boiling water, clover, quarantine, germ theory, vaccination, Arabic numerals | Spreads freely. Healthier neighbors make better customers and fewer plagues ship in, and good ideas help the hero's reputation |
| **Sell the product, keep the method** | Spectacles, clear glass, paper, cloth finishing, spirits | Patents in England, trade secrets in the workshop. Rivals copy within 5–15 years; by then the Company is two steps on |
| **Keep at home** | **Machine tools**, precision gauges, coke smelting, steam engines, the schools' methods | The hardest to copy, because copying needs **a whole system of trained people**, not one clever spy |

**Tricks that keep the lead:**
- **Run, don't hide.** Always have the next improvement ready before the last one leaks.
- **The people are the secret.** Pay well, give skilled workers **shares** (they own what a rival would steal), pensions, schooling for their children, and houses in Company towns. A worker who'd lose all that won't sell out to Bruges.
- **Coal is the moat.** Flanders and northern France have little coal near water *(the Liège coal field exists but is smaller and inland)*. A coal economy is hard to copy without coal.
- **Copying is also growth.** If France copies spectacles, more people read. Tim's goal is to develop people, and England is just the group the hero can steer.

## Story texture
**Tensions:**
- **Rich but never safe.** He can buy anything except the right to keep it. Every king could seize it all, and he knows what happened to the Riccardi and the Bardi.
- **Neutral in a civil war.** Montfort is the most impressive man he meets and his ideas (a parliament with towns) are the closest to the hero's. Not backing him costs the hero something personal.
- **The copying race.** A friend from Bruges, a Flemish master weaver, takes the Company's spinning secret home. Is it betrayal, or exactly what the world needed?
- **Smoke and progress.** Coal makes London warm and rich and also chokes it. In history, Queen Eleanor left Nottingham Castle in 1257 because of the coal smoke.
- **The founder who will miss his own payoff.** He spends his last years writing sealed orders for a plague he'll never see.

**Real people he'd meet:**
- **Robert Grosseteste**: the one he tells. A bishop who grinds lenses and asks hard questions.
- **Roger Bacon**: the restless genius who finally gets a lab and the funding.
- **Matthew Paris**, the St Albans chronicler and gossip, who writes down the strange merchant (and gets things wrong).
- **Richard of Cornwall**: investor, rival and fellow money-man.
- **Henry III**: kind, extravagant, building **Westminster Abbey** from 1245. The hero could help engineer it.
- **Simon de Montfort** and his wife **Eleanor**: charisma and danger.
- **The Lord Edward**, later Edward I: the administrator king who finally understands what the Company is.
- **Walter de Merton**: a fellow builder of institutions.
- **Andrew Bukerel**, the mayor and pepperer: maybe his first partner.

**Scenes:**
- A tally stick snapped in the Exchequer, then the hero's double-entry ledger laid beside it.
- Grosseteste putting on the first pair of spectacles and reading a page he hadn't been able to read for ten years.
- The sealed letter, opened years later in Lincoln.
- **1258:** Baltic grain ships coming up the Thames while the city starves, and the Company's kitchens boiling water.
- **The day after Evesham:** both sides' men eating at the same Company table.
- The first **steam pump** in a flooded Cornish tin mine: the water falls, and the miners go quiet.
- A Flemish spy hired, caught, and then offered a better job.
- The old man's last Governors' meeting, handing over the Founder's Books.
- **1348:** a plague ship turned away at a Company wharf, with the founder's instruction read aloud.

**Strengths:**
- **Everything practical is close together:** coal, iron, tin, water power, timber, wool and the sea.
- **A literate, legal, money-using society already exists.** He doesn't have to invent writing, coins or markets; he improves them.
- **People and skills can be bought nearby**, and Europe's scholars are a few days away.
- **This route avoids the throne problem.** A merchant doesn't need to beat anyone to rise.
- The aim is the **real Industrial Revolution's own birthplace**, just centuries early.

**Weaknesses:**
- **He controls only his Company.** England's kings can still go to war, tax and seize.
- **Copying:** every lead is temporary.
- **Room to grow is small:** a full island, so growth eventually means ships (Ireland, and later the Atlantic).
- **Political risk is constant**, from 1258 through 1265, 1294 and 1327.
- **Slower to steer** than the variations where he leads.
