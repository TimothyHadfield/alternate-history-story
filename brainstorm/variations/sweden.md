# Sweden: the Man Behind the Jarl

*One man tells one ruler the truth, then builds a cold, small country into an iron-and-water powerhouse from behind the throne.*

**This is a variation for comparison, not a decision.** Nothing here is chosen. Facts are mostly from memory; the ones checked on the web are listed under Sources, and doubtful ones are marked *(uncertain)*.

## What makes this one different
- **The secret-partner route.** He never rules in his own name and never founds a state. He tells **one man, Birger Magnusson (later Birger Jarl)**, the truth, proves it once, and co-rules through him. The other five variations found, marry in, advise or unify. This one **borrows a throne that is about to become strong**.
- **Iron, copper, wood and water instead of good soil.** Sweden has some of the purest iron ore in Europe (Bergslagen), Europe's greatest copper mine (Falun), **endless timber and a water wheel site on every stream**. It has **no coal** and **poor, cold farmland**. So growth is driven by **metals and water power**, and food has to be engineered.
- **A cold-climate food system.** Every other variation has warm or rich farmland. Here the main job is **keeping people and cattle alive through long winters** and getting ready for the **Little Ice Age** (from ~1300).
- **A small population is the hard limit.** Sweden had about **0.5–1 million people**. Growth needs **births plus steady immigration** (Germans, Finns, Balts, refugees), not conquest.
- **A partner, not a rival, to a rising trade network.** The **Hanseatic League** (Lübeck, Visby) is forming right next door. This variation **rides it**: Stockholm becomes the Hansa's best customer and supplier, and German merchants become Swedes.

## The setup
**Arrival:** spring **1240**, in **Östergötland**, near **Bjälbo**, the home estate of the Folkung family (*the exact spot is a story choice*).

**The world around him:**
- **King Erik Eriksson** ("the Lisp and Lame") is back on the throne since 1234. He is weak and childless. Real power sits with the great families.
- **The jarl** (the king's top man) is **Ulf Fase**. **Birger Magnusson**, about 30 *(birth year uncertain, c. 1210)*, is a rising magnate. He is **married to Ingeborg, the king's sister**, so his sons have a claim to the throne.
- In July 1240 Swedes fight **Alexander of Novgorod at the Neva**. Russian tradition says Birger led them *(uncertain; Swedish sources don't confirm it)*.
- Sweden is a loose union of provinces (Svealand around Lake Mälaren, Götaland in the south). Each has its own **law and assembly (the ting)** and its **lawspeaker**. **Free farmers carry arms and vote at the ting.** There are no great feudal lords like in France.
- **Skåne (Scania) is Danish.** So are the great herring markets at Skanör. Gotland, with its trading town **Visby**, is semi-independent.
- **Lübeck** (an imperial free city since 1226) is becoming the hub of the German trade that will become the Hanseatic League.

**What he has:** nothing but what's in his head. He's an Electrical Engineering student. He knows physics, chemistry basics, circuits, how machines work, and modern ideas about germs, diet and farming. No tools, no money, no friends.

**The people:** farmers, fishermen, charcoal burners, miners in Bergslagen, a few town traders, and many **thralls (slaves)** still. Practical people who respect skill. A foreigner stays an outsider for a long time (acceptance 5/10).

**The language:** **Old Swedish** (Old East Norse). English speakers have a head start: many basic words are related (hus/house, kona/woman, fisk/fish). Expect **6–12 months** to talk, 2 years to argue a case at the ting. **Low German** is the trade language and is worth learning second. Latin only through clergy, and he doesn't need it.

## The rise to power
| Year | Step | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1240 | **Arrive, survive, work** | Takes work at a farm or forge near Bjälbo. Learns the language. Fixes things. |
| 1240–41 | **Small proofs** | A **water-powered saw** at a mill (a sawblade on a crank, worked by the wheel). A **better stove** that heats a room on a third of the wood. **Soap** from wood ash and fat. A **cool store of ice and sawdust** that keeps fish fresh into summer. People start coming to see. |
| 1241 | **Noticed by the Folkungs** | Birger's steward brings him in. Birger is a builder and organizer by nature. He sees a man who makes **wood go further and mills do more**. |
| Late 1241 | **The one secret proof** | Alone with Birger, he tells the truth and hands him **one sealed letter**. It says: (1) news will come this year that a great army from the east destroyed the Polish and Hungarian hosts in spring 1241 (Legnica and Mohi, April 1241, news that reaches Sweden through Lübeck months later); (2) **Birger will become jarl, his son will be king, and he will rule Sweden.** Part 1 is checked within months. Part 2 is a promise Birger can hold him to. **This is the only prediction he ever makes, to anyone.** |
| 1242–47 | **The quiet partner** | Birger gives him land near a waterfall and a share in his Bergslagen interests. Officially he is **"Birger's master of works"**, a foreign craftsman. In private they plan. |
| 1247 | **The Sparrsätra uprising** (Holger Knutsson's revolt) | He keeps his works out of it. It's the first test of whether Birger trusts him with more than mills. |
| **1248** | **Birger becomes jarl** | The letter comes true. From here, Birger listens. |
| **1250** | **King Erik dies. Birger's son Valdemar (about 11) is elected king. Birger rules as regent.** | **This is the moment he holds real power**: Birger's closest adviser, with control of **mines, mint, works and the new town**. |
| 1251 | Battle of **Herrevadsbro** | The last big Folkung rebellion is crushed. Birger has the leaders executed despite a safe-conduct. The partner has to live with that. |
| **1252** | **Stockholm** | First written mention, July 1252. In this variation it is **his project**: a fortified town at the outlet of Lake Mälaren, with the toll gate and the strong current at **Norrström** for water wheels. Same year: the **trade treaty with Lübeck**. |
| 1252–66 | **Co-rule** | Birger's laws (peace laws for home, church, ting and women; an end to trial by ordeal; daughters inherit) go out under Birger's name, with the partner's ideas folded in. |
| **1266** | **Birger dies** (October 1266, Västergötland) | The test of whether what they built **outlives the man in front**. |

**Risks along the way:**
- **Birger is ruthless.** He keeps promises when they suit him. If the partner becomes a danger, he can be dropped.
- **"Sorcerer" whispers.** A foreigner who makes ice in summer and wood that burns twice as long draws talk. So everything is shown as **craft and teaching**, never magic.
- **The magnates.** They pick kings. If they see a foreigner steering the jarl, they turn on both. So he holds **works, not titles**.
- **Valdemar is weak.** History has his brother **Magnus** overthrow him in **1275**. The partner needs a plan that survives that (see Succession).

## How the group grows
**Starting point:** about **0.6–0.75 million** in Sweden proper in 1240 *(uncertain; estimates for the 1200s range ~0.5–1M)*, plus Swedish-settled coasts of Finland.

**Population sources:**
| Source | How | Rough numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **Births surviving** | The biggest lever. Cut infant and mother deaths: clean birth (boiled cloths, washed hands), feeding help, warm dry houses, no winter famine | Growth from ~0.2% to **~1% a year** |
| **German immigrants** | The **Lübeck treaty of 1252** lets German merchants who settle in Sweden become Swedes. Add miners and smiths for Bergslagen (they came historically in the 1300s). Offer land, mine shares, tax-free years | **~20,000–40,000** over 50 years |
| **Finns and Karelians** | Already partly under Swedish rule. Invite them to clear forest land and burn charcoal (they were famous for slash-and-burn forest farming) | **~30,000–60,000** |
| **Refugees** | **Old Prussians** fleeing the Teutonic Order's conquest (the Great Prussian Uprising 1260–74); Estonians and Livonians; Frisian farmers after the **St Lucia's flood (1287)**; later Norwegian and Danish famine refugees | **Tens of thousands**, in waves |
| **Neighbors joining** | Gotland's farmers through trade ties; **Norrland's coasts** (Hälsingland, Ångermanland) settled northward; peaceful trade with the **Sámi** | New land, not new conquests |

**Target numbers:** **1.0M by 1270, 1.4M by 1300, 2M+ by 1350**, keeping food and housing per person rising the whole time (Tim's "maximum population growth as well as maximum immigration while maintaining positive growth").

**How food, housing and health keep up:**
- **Food first, immigrants second.** Each wave arrives after a good harvest, to land already cleared and a store already full.
- **Spread newcomers through villages**, not into enclaves. Germans go to towns and mines, Finns to the forest frontier, Prussians to farms.
- **Housing is easy here:** timber is endless. A water-powered sawmill turns out boards fast. Add **better stoves, double doors, moss-and-clay insulation**.
- **Room to grow:** Norrland's river valleys are nearly empty and full of timber and water power.

## Government and society
**How it's run in this variation:** **Birger rules, the partner runs the works.** The machinery of Swedish society stays: the **king, the council of magnates, the provincial things and lawspeakers**. Underneath, the partner builds a second system:
- **The Works (Bruken).** A chartered company for each district's mines, furnaces, saws and mills, with **shares owned by the crown, Birger, the partner and the miners themselves**. This fits Sweden perfectly: the **Falun copper mountain was already run as a shared cooperative of miner-owners** (the oldest known share, 1288). It also makes the partner's power look like **business**, not politics.
- **Honest measures.** One set of weights, lengths and measures, kept in Stockholm and copied to every town. A mint with **coins of fixed silver content**, plus iron bars of fixed weight used as money in the north.
- **Written land records.** Who owns what, measured once. It ends land quarrels and makes fair taxes possible.
- **Feedback.** The **ting already lets free farmers speak and vote**. The partner uses it: every new rule is tried in one province first, and the lawspeakers report back what worked.

**How it fits the local culture:** Swedes **respect law, assemblies and practical skill**. Things are pitched as **old freedoms made stronger**: a farmer's right to his land written down, his right to a share of the mine he works. **Thralldom** fades by being made **uneconomic** (free workers with shares outproduce thralls). Birger's peace laws already point that way, and historically thralldom was abolished in 1335.

**Schools:**
- First, **an apprentice school at each works**: reading, sums with **Arabic numerals**, drawing, and the craft.
- Teaching in **Swedish written in the Latin alphabet**, with spelling simplified. Runes are still in use, so literacy is already familiar to some.
- By 1270, **a teaching hall in Stockholm** for engineers, surveyors, healers and clerks. Later (1290s), something like a university, though that word isn't needed.
- **Paper from wood pulp** (see the tech path) makes books cheap enough for every village.

**Succession (he plans for his own death even though he won't die soon):**
- **The partnership must outlive Birger (1266).** From 1250 he trains **Birger's sons** alongside the apprentices. **Magnus** (born c. 1240, the future Magnus Ladulås) is the capable one.
- **Make the system not need him.** The Works' charters, the measures, the land records and the schools all run under written rules and councils.
- **A named heir of skill**, not blood: his best-trained pupil, who knows the truth. A second sealed letter, this one with plans and not predictions.
- **The crown keeps the throne; the Works keep the knowledge.** Whoever wins in 1275 inherits a machine that pays him. So no king has a reason to break it.

## The tech path
**Local resources and where they are:**
| Resource | Where |
|---|---|
| **Iron ore** (low phosphorus, makes excellent steel) | **Bergslagen**: Norberg, Västmanland, Dalarna. Later **Dannemora** (north of Uppsala), the purest of all |
| **Copper** | **Falun, the "Great Copper Mountain"** (Stora Kopparberget), Dalarna |
| **Timber, charcoal, tar** | Everywhere, especially Dalarna, Norrland, Finland |
| **Water power** | Every river: **Dalälven, Norrström at Stockholm, Motala ström**, Göta älv |
| **Lime** | Gotland, Öland, Västergötland |
| **Peat** | Bogs everywhere (a second fuel) |
| **Coal** | **None in Swedish hands** (Skåne's small coal is Danish). Bought from **Newcastle** later |

| Phase | Key tasks and inventions | Why this order, for Sweden |
|---|---|---|
| **Years 0–2** (1240–42) | **Water-powered sawmill**; **efficient masonry stove** (a long smoke path through brick, heats all night); **soap**; **ice house** with sawdust; **root cellars**; wheelbarrow; better charcoal kilns (covered stacks); gathering **wild clover seed** | Wood and cold are the two facts of Swedish life. Saving fuel and stretching the winter food supply pay off for **every household at once**, with nothing new needed |
| **Years 3–5** (1243–45) | **Upgrade Bergslagen's blast furnaces** (Lapphyttan-type, already working): taller stacks, **water-driven bellows** everywhere, better ore roasting; **finery forges** to turn pig iron into bar iron; **Falun pumps and hoists** driven by water wheels; standard measures | Sweden **already has blast furnaces** (Lapphyttan, 1100s–1300s). Improving a working one is fast. Iron is the money that pays for everything else |
| **Years 6–10** (1246–50) | **Steel** (cementation: bar iron baked with charcoal), **iron ploughshares and scythes for every farm**, **nails and wire** (water-powered drawing), **turnip and clover rotation**, **tar kilns** at scale, cart roads and **winter sledge routes** for ore | Cheap iron tools raise every farm's output. Steel scythes cut more hay, so **more cattle survive winter**, which means more manure and milk. Tar is the Baltic's top ship supply and an easy export |
| **Years 11–20** (1251–60) | **Stockholm** with water wheels at Norrström; **better ships** (stern rudder, magnetic compass, three masts later); **glass** (windows and bottles: sand, lime, potash); **wood-pulp paper** (a water-powered grindstone pressing logs, then a screen); **printing** with cast type (Falun copper and lead/tin); grain stores for 1258 | Stockholm is the **gate** for all of Mälaren's trade, so its works and port come first. **Wood-pulp paper is the big "nobody thought of it" idea for Sweden**: Europe made paper from rags, which Sweden lacks, but it has endless wood |
| **Years 21–50** (1261–90) | **Water turbines** (much more power than wheels); **rolling and slitting mills**; **wooden then iron-capped rails** from mines to lakes; **pound locks and canals** (Mälaren to Hjälmaren, toward the west coast); **cowpox vaccination**; a **wood-fired steam engine** for mine pumping where there's no stream; **the first voyage west** via Norway, Iceland and Greenland (the Norse route still works) | Turbines and rails multiply what the mines can ship. Canals tie the lakes into one network. Steam comes **late** because water power is so plentiful; it's needed only where water isn't |
| **Years 51–100** (1291–1340) | **Electricity**: generators from **Falun copper wire and Bergslagen iron**, driven by water turbines; **telegraph** from Stockholm to Uppsala, Falun and the coast; **electric arc furnaces** for steel; air-blown steelmaking (Bessemer-style works well with low-phosphorus Swedish ore); **steam ships**; **new crops from the Americas** settled in; chemistry for fertilizer | This is where the EE student's own field pays off. **Sweden is unusually good for early electricity**: copper, iron and water power in the same province. Electric steel makes **coal unnecessary** |

## Food, water and health
**Water** is not the problem: lakes and rivers are clean and everywhere. The first tasks are about **cold, short summers and long winters**.

**First tasks (Years 0–5):**
1. **Root cellars** in every farm: dug into a hillside, kept just above freezing. Turnips, cabbage and stored grain last all winter.
2. **Ice houses** with sawdust from the new sawmills. Cut lake ice in February, and **fish and meat keep into summer without salt**. This matters because **salt must be imported**.
3. **More hay, more cattle.** Steel scythes, **clover and turnip rotation** (rye, turnips, barley, clover). Clover puts nitrogen back in the soil and feeds cattle. Historically, cattle starving in late winter was a yearly crisis.
4. **Salt.** Buy it from **Lüneburg via Lübeck**. Also make some: on the **west coast** (saltier sea than the Baltic), **freeze sea water in winter**. The ice that forms is mostly fresh, so the leftover brine is much saltier and takes far less wood to boil down.
5. **Herring.** Local Baltic herring (strömming) plus the huge Scania herring markets. Preserve it with less salt: **smoking, drying, fermenting, and ice**.
6. **Grain reserves.** Crown granaries in every province, holding a full year, built **stone-floored and rat-proof**.

**Crops:**
| Crop | Status in 1240 | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Rye** (winter rye) | Grown | The main bread grain; hardy |
| **Barley, oats** | Grown | Short season, beer, horse feed |
| **Turnips** | Grown in small amounts | Push hard: winter food for people and cattle |
| **Clover** | Grows wild | **Gather the seed and sow it on purpose**: a simple idea with big effects |
| **Peas, beans, cabbage** | Grown | Cabbage becomes **sauerkraut** (needs little salt; prevents scurvy) |
| **Potatoes** | **In the Andes only** | Would need an **Atlantic and Pacific voyage** to South America. A Years 51–100 goal. Note: the potatoes that suit northern Europe best come from **southern Chile**, where the days are long *(reasoned)* |
| **Jerusalem artichoke** | In North America | Cold-hardy, and closer: the Norse route to Newfoundland and the St Lawrence. A Years 21–50 voyage |
| **Buckwheat** | Not yet in Sweden | Grows on poor soil in a short season; can be bought from Eastern Europe |

**Health:**
- **Clean childbirth** and **boiled water for wounds**: the cheapest way to raise the population.
- **Soap and the sauna** (the bathhouse is already a Nordic habit, so hygiene sells easily).
- **Scurvy:** sauerkraut, lingonberries, cloudberries, spruce-needle tea through the winter.
- **Rickets and dark winters:** **cod liver oil** and fatty fish (vitamin D).
- **Smallpox:** **cowpox vaccination** once he can show it safely (cattle are everywhere).
- **Plague preparation (for 1350):** **rat-proof stone granaries**, cats, cleaner towns, a **quarantine system for ships** (40 days, which Ragusa invented in 1377), and isolating the sick.

## Trade and the outside world
**The gate: Stockholm.** All of Lake Mälaren's trade (Uppsala, Västerås, the Bergslagen iron and the Falun copper) passes through one narrow outlet. Whoever holds it holds the toll. Second gates: **Kalmar** (southeast), **Lödöse** (west coast, toward Norway and England), and **Visby** (Gotland) as a partner.

| Goes out | Comes in |
|---|---|
| **Iron**: first osmund lumps, then bar iron, then **finished steel tools, nails, wire** | **Salt** (Lüneburg, later the Bay of Biscay) |
| **Copper**: bars, then sheet and wire | **Grain** in bad years (Prussia, Poland via Danzig) |
| **Tar, pitch, boards, masts** | **Cloth** (Flanders, England) |
| Butter, hides, **furs** (via Finland and the Sámi) | **Wine, spices** (luxuries, kept small) |
| Later: **paper, glass, printed books** | **Coal** from Newcastle, once ships are big enough |
| | **Skilled people**: miners, glassmakers, masons, scholars |

**The Hanseatic League: ride it, don't fight it.**
- The **Lübeck treaty of 1252** made Stockholm Lübeck's chief market in Sweden. Here it's pushed further: **German merchants who settle become Swedish citizens**, sit on the town council, and bring their capital and trade links.
- Sweden **sells finished goods, not raw ore**, so the value stays home even if Hansa ships carry it.
- **Visby** and **Lübeck** stay friendly: Swedish iron and copper make Hansa merchants rich, so they have every reason to keep the Baltic peaceful.

**Secrets kept or let go:**
| Let it spread | Keep inside the Works |
|---|---|
| Stoves, root cellars, ice houses, soap, clover, sauerkraut, clean childbirth, vaccination, honest measures | Steelmaking details, furnace and turbine design, **wood-pulp paper**, glass recipes, printing type, later electricity |
| *Why:* they help everyone, they can't be stopped, and they make neighbors richer trade partners | *Why:* they are what Sweden sells. Masters are paid in shares, so leaving costs them. Workshops split each process so no single worker knows it all |

## Story texture
**Tensions:**
- **Is he Birger's friend or Birger's tool?** Birger executes the Herrevadsbro rebels after promising them safety. The partner sees what his patron is capable of, and knows the same could happen to him.
- **Credit.** Every law and every town goes out under Birger's name. The partner builds a country and is remembered as a craftsman.
- **The brothers.** He tutors both Valdemar and Magnus. He knows (and Birger doesn't) that Magnus will take his brother's throne in 1275. Does he tell anyone? He promised only one prediction.
- **Secrets vs. sharing.** Germans join the Works and might carry the steel secret home to Lübeck. How tightly can a small country hold its edge, and should it?
- **Cold.** The Little Ice Age is coming, and he can't say so. He has to convince farmers to store grain for a famine nobody else expects.
- **Birger's death in 1266.** The day the man in front is gone.

**Real people he'd meet:**
- **Birger Jarl** (c. 1210–1266) and **Ingeborg Eriksdotter** (the king's sister, his wife, died 1254).
- **King Erik Eriksson** (died 1250). **King Valdemar** (reigned 1250–75). **Magnus Ladulås** (king 1275–90, "barn lock", named for protecting farmers from lords).
- **Birger's daughter Rikissa**, married to **Haakon the Young** of Norway (1251).
- **Haakon IV of Norway**, who makes peace with Birger.
- **Alexander Nevsky** of Novgorod, across the eastern border.
- Lübeck's council merchants; Gotland's farmer-traders; the miner-owners of Falun; Finnish forest settlers; Sámi traders in the north.
- Later: **Torgils Knutsson** (regent 1290s, builder of Viborg castle) and **Magnus Eriksson** (king from 1319, who issued the Falun charter in 1347).

**Scenes:**
- The first winter: a foreigner who can't speak, fixing a broken mill wheel for a meal.
- **Ice cut from the lake in February, fish served fresh at midsummer**, and the village elders not believing it.
- Birger breaking the seal on the letter in 1248, the day he's named jarl.
- **The first night in Stockholm**, a log palisade on an island, the current roaring past the new water wheels.
- **The summer of 1258**: grey skies, frost in July, and the partner's granaries opening while the rest of Europe goes hungry.
- Ore sledges in a long line over a frozen lake at dawn.
- Birger's funeral at **Varnhem**, and the partner alone with the second letter he wrote for his own heir.
- A glowing wire in a dark hall at Falun, the first electric light, powered by the river.
- 1350: a plague ship turned away at the Stockholm harbor chain.

## Strengths and weaknesses
**Ratings from [location-factors.md](../location-factors.md) and [location-shortlist.md](../location-shortlist.md):** practical resources **7**, valuables 7, population and settlers **6**, acceptance **5**, safety **6**; land average **6.8** (water 10, climate **3**, disasters 6, food **4**, imports 7, health 7, energy 9, transport 8, building 8, room 6); ease of rising to power **3**. **Overall 5.7/10.**

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| **The best iron ore in Europe and its biggest copper mine**, both already mined, so experts and workers exist | **No coal**: charcoal and electricity have to carry the industry, and coal must be bought |
