# Variation 5. Andes: the Organizer

*Alone on a continent with no iron, no horses and no writing, he unites a hundred quarrelling valleys by making them all richer together, two centuries before the Inca.*

This is **one variation, written for comparison**. It is not a decision. Nothing about the story is chosen.

## What makes this one different
- **Total isolation.** No Old World crops, animals, books, iron or craftsmen can reach him, ever, in his lifetime. **Everything is built from Andean resources.** None of the other five starts this cut off.
- **He joins people up instead of beating them.** In the Andes, a leader's power comes from **how much he can give**: food, cloth, feasts and help in bad years. He becomes the best giver because he grows the biggest surplus. Valleys join his **storehouse network** because being outside it is riskier.
- **A planned command economy from scratch.** The Andes already run on labor, not money: the **ayllu** (kin group), **mit'a** (turn-based work duty) and **qollqa** (storehouses). He upgrades the system he finds instead of bringing in a foreign one.
- **Tim's government idea fits best here.** Total final power plus feedback systems is close to how the later Inca state worked, minus the weak points that let it collapse in 1532.
- **The longest safe runway.** No outside rival with ships or steel until 1492 at the earliest. He has **260 years** to prepare a continent for first contact, including its biggest danger: **Old World disease**.

## The setup
**Arrival:** **1230, the west shore of Lake Titicaca**, in the lands of the **Lupaca** (Aymara speakers, around modern Puno and Chucuito, about 3,800 m up).

**Why here and not Cusco:** the Titicaca basin is the **most crowded, richest highland region** of the time. It has the largest llama and alpaca herds, lake fish, reeds, potatoes and quinoa, the ruins of **Tiwanaku** (a great state that fell around 1000–1100), and **tin** within reach to the south-east (around Oruro, in modern Bolivia). Cusco in 1230 is a **small kingdom** in one valley. The Titicaca lords are bigger.

**The world around him (the "Late Intermediate Period"):**
| Who | Where | What they're like in 1230 |
|---|---|---|
| **Lupaca** | South-west shore of Titicaca; seat at **Cutimbo** | Aymara herding lords, huge herds. Later capital Chucuito |
| **Colla** | North-west shore (Hatuncolla area) | Aymara; **bitter rivals of the Lupaca** |
| **Pacajes, Charcas and others** | South and east of the lake | Smaller Aymara lordships |
| **Cusco (early Inca)** | ~400 km north-west | A minor kingdom. King lists put **Sinchi Roca or Lloque Yupanqui** around now (**legendary dates**) |
| **Chanka; Huanca** | West of Cusco; Mantaro valley | A warlike confederation; dense farming valleys |
| **Chimú (Chimor)** | North coast, capital **Chan Chan** (near Trujillo) | The one real state: canals, crafts, a king. Expanding along the coast |
| **Lambayeque; Chincha** | Far north coast; south-central coast | Bronze metalworkers; seafaring traders |

**The mood:** small lordships, **hilltop forts (pucaras)**, raiding over herds and pasture, and no one in charge. Scholars debate how unified even the "kingdoms" were (Arkush argues they were fragmented). **That fragmentation is his opening.**

**What he has:** nothing but knowledge. No tools, no metal, no seeds, no paper. Just an EE student's head: math, physics, basic chemistry, how a generator works, germ theory, what crops grow where, and a rough map of where things are.

**The language:** **Aymara** at the lake, **Puquina** in pockets around it, and **Quechua** dialects to the north (it is not yet the empire-wide language; *where Quechua was spoken in 1230 is uncertain*). He learns Aymara first, then Quechua. **Both languages have simple, regular syllables**, which matters later: they're easy to write with a syllable script.

**What already exists (big head starts):**
- **Quipu**, knotted cords that record numbers in **base 10, with place value** (zero is an empty spot on the cord).
- **Terraces, canals, raised fields**, and freeze-dried **chuño** that keeps for years.
- **Rope bridges, llama caravans**, and a culture of **shared community work**.

## The rise to power
**The route: steward of the storehouses.** Not king, prophet or general. He becomes the man who **makes sure nobody goes hungry**, and that job grows until it is the state.

| Year | Step | Proof or move |
|---|---|---|
| **1230** | **Quarantine first.** Before meeting anyone he lives apart for **about 3 weeks** in an empty herding hut, boiling water and watching for a cough. He knows his body carries modern colds, flu strains and gut bugs these people have never met (*reasoned, see Health*) | No proof yet. Just not killing his first hosts |
| 1230–31 | Taken in by **one ayllu** as a strange outsider. Labors in their fields, learns Aymara. Asks endless questions | Earns a place through **ayni** (help given, help returned) |
| **1231** | First proofs: **boiled water and salt-and-honey drinks** stop children dying of diarrhea. Better chuño drying. **Soap** from fat and quinoa-stalk ash | Mothers become his first allies |
| **1232** | Revives **raised fields (suka kollus)** on the flat lake shore: earth platforms with water channels between them. The water holds the day's heat and **shields crops from frost** | The ayllu's harvest is the best around in a frost year. **This is the big visible proof** |
| 1233–34 | The local **mallku** (lord; *his real name is unknown, so the story invents him*) asks him to run his **storehouses and quipus** | He adds **standard measures**, a census, and storehouses built to stay cold and dry |
| **1235** | A bad frost or drought year hits the lake. His lord's storehouses **feed neighbors too**. In the Andes, the giver gains authority over the fed | **Three neighboring ayllus join** the lord's network |
| 1236–38 | Runs the lord's **mit'a**. Builds a **graded road**, raised fields and a canal by turn-based labor. Marries into the mallku's family (Andean alliances run through marriage) | Becomes the lord's heir in all but name |
| **~1240** | The old lord names him **co-ruler**; the **Lupaca** lordships unite under one storehouse network | **Real power.** He is the redistributor-in-chief of perhaps 50,000–100,000 people (*a guess*) |
| 1240s | Offers the **Colla** not war but **membership**: shared storehouses, shared pasture rules, raised-field crews, bronze tools | The Lupaca–Colla feud ends inside one system |
| **1250s** | The whole **Titicaca basin** is in the network. Delegations come from **Cusco** and the Quechua valleys to learn | **The Organizer** is now the biggest power in the southern Andes |

**One secret proof (optional).** If the story wants it: he tells the full truth to **one person only**, his wife or the old mallku, and proves it with something that can be checked, like the date of a coming eclipse. Nobody else hears it. In public, he is simply **the man whose storehouses never run empty**.

**Risks in the rise:**
- **He sickens his hosts.** The quarantine is the fix, but a mild cold could still start an epidemic.
- **Rival lords see him as a threat.** Fix: he always rules *through* a local lord at first, and every new valley keeps its own kuraka (headman).
- **Stranger magic.** An outsider who changes everything can be feared. He frames everything as **Andean**: reviving Tiwanaku's raised fields, upgrading the quipu, honoring ayni. Old ways, done better.

## How the group grows
**Population sources, in order of size:**
1. **Neighbors joining.** By far the biggest. The Andes held **millions of people** (*estimates for 1500 run from about 6 to 15 million, so 1230 is a guess*). Most of his growth is whole valleys signing up.
2. **Births.** Fewer children and mothers die once water, soap, clean births and stored food arrive.
3. **Refugees.** Groups pushed off their pasture by raids or drought come to the network for food. He settles them **spread through existing ayllus**, not in camps.
4. **Herders and coastal people.** Later, coast fishermen and Amazon-edge farmers.

**Why valleys join (the "insurance" pitch):** every valley loses a harvest some years. **Join and nobody starves.** You get bronze tools, seed potatoes and healers, and your kuraka keeps his post. Outside, you face the next bad year alone.

**Target numbers (guesses):**
| Year | People in the network |
|---|---|
| 1235 | ~2,000 (a few ayllus) |
| 1240 | ~75,000 (the Lupaca) |
| 1255 | ~400,000 (the Titicaca basin) |
| 1275 | ~1.5 million (southern highlands plus Cusco) |
| 1300 | ~3 million (plus the central valleys) |
| 1330 | ~5 million (plus a partnership with the coast) |
| 1400 | ~9–10 million (natural growth near 1.5–2% a year) |

**Keeping food, housing and health ahead of numbers** (Tim's rule: max growth, max immigration, but never let food per person fall):
- **Storehouses lead.** No valley joins until its first-year food is stored. Rule of thumb: **two years of grain and chuño per person** held in reserve.
- **Raised fields and terraces first.** Each new valley's first mit'a is food land, then a road, then a school.
- **Housing:** adobe and stone already work. He adds **earthquake-safe rules** (see Hazards) and **chimneys** (smoky huts cause lung disease).

## Government and society
**The basic shape: Tim's idea, built on Andean bones.** One founder with **total final authority**, plus **feedback systems** that tell him the truth. Most of the parts already exist in some form; he adds the feedback.

| Andean piece that exists | What he adds |
|---|---|
| **Ayllu**: kin groups that share land and work | Stays the base unit. Each ayllu runs its own fields and keeps what it grows above the shared quota |
| **Kuraka**: local headmen | Keep their posts (that's why valleys join). Trained, then **promoted by results**: harvest, child survival, roads kept |
| **Mit'a**: turn-based labor duty | The main "tax". Every adult gives some weeks a year. **Now it also teaches**: crews learn masonry, bronze casting, surveying |
| **Qollqa**: storehouses | Counted twice a year. **The core of the state** |
| **Quipu and quipu-keepers** | Become a **statistics office**. Two independent counts, by two separate offices, are compared |
| **Reciprocity (ayni)** | He keeps it as the moral center: the state gives first (food, tools, healing), then asks for work |

**The feedback systems (what stops total power from going blind):**
- **Two counts of everything.** Harvests, births and deaths are knotted by the ayllu **and** by a state counter. If they don't match, an inspector visits. Faking a quipu is the one serious crime for officials.
- **Inspectors who "see all."** A small corps that answers only to him and visits valleys unannounced (the later Inca had a similar post, the *tocricoc*). **Their job is to find problems, and they are praised for it.**
- **Reward bad news, punish hiding.** A kuraka who reports a failed harvest early gets help. One who hides it loses his post. This is the fix for the Great Leap Forward trap in [government.md](../government.md).
- **The runner post as a petition line.** Relay runners (**chasqui**) carry quipus and messages. Any ayllu can send a knotted petition up the line and get an answer.
- **Test small first.** Every new method (a crop, a tool, a rule) runs in **two or three pilot ayllus** for a season before it spreads.
- **People whose job is to argue with him.** A small council of the sharpest kurakas and students, told plainly: *your job is to say where I'm wrong.*

**Where he keeps central control vs. lets go:**
| The state runs | People run, under state rules |
|---|---|
| Storehouses, roads, bridges, canals | Fields, herds and fishing |
| Mines, smelters, later the ironworks | Weaving, pottery and crafts at home |
| Schools, healers, the runner post | Local trade and barter markets |
| Research workshops, the first press | Inventions (rewarded with prizes and fame) |

**Fitting the local culture:** he is the **chief steward**, first among kurakas, never a god or prophet. He speaks the languages, keeps local festivals and gives the biggest feasts.

**Schools:**
- **Quipu schools** first (Year 3+): counting, measuring, record-keeping, and soon reading. Students are chosen from every ayllu, **girls included**.
- **Mit'a as school:** every adult's work turn teaches a skill.
- **A central academy** at the lake by about 1250: surveying, medicine, metallurgy, astronomy, and the **new writing**.

**Succession (planned from the start; he's healthy, but he plans anyway):**
- **Years 0–15:** a written fallback. If he dies, power goes to a **council of the senior kurakas plus his chosen steward**, who holds the storehouse quipus.
- **Years 15–40:** he trains **two or three successor candidates** (one may be his child, but it isn't required). Each runs a region for years. **They are judged by the numbers.**
- **Years 40+:** written laws that bind even the ruler. The council chooses the next steward from the candidates. **The system must survive a weak heir.**
- **The Inca lesson:** the Inca state fell in months in 1532 because everything ran through **one captured man** and a civil war between two heirs. His design: **no single point of failure**. Regional storehouse capitals can run alone for years. The law, the quipu offices and the council continue whoever is on top.

## The tech path
**Why this order, for this place:** food and health first (they buy loyalty and population). Then **counting and writing** (the system is only as good as its records). Then **better bronze**, because copper and tin are close and iron is far. **Iron and coal come late**, because Andean iron ore and coal are far from the highland core and need a coastal partner. Transport improves all along, because it is the Andes' worst weakness.

**Local resources and where they are:**
| Resource | Where | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Tin** | Bolivian highlands (Oruro to Potosí area) | Tin bronze: harder tools than copper |
| **Copper** | Many places; big later mines in southern Peru and Chile | Bronze, wire, later electrical parts |
| **Coal (anthracite)** | **Santa valley and Alto Chicama**, northern Peru, near the Chimú. The Chimú may have burned some (*a 2004 study found possible coal ash at Chan Chan*) | Smelting iron, glass, kilns, later steam |
| **Iron ore** | **Marcona** on the south coast (huge), plus many smaller highland deposits | Iron, later steel |
| **Guano** | **Chincha Islands** and other islands off the coast | The world's best fertilizer |
| **Nitrates** | **Atacama** (Tarapacá), far south | Fertilizer, blasting powder for roads and mines |
| **Salt** | **Uyuni** salt flats, coast salt pans | Preserving food, health |
| **Cotton** | Coastal valleys (native long-staple cotton) | Cloth, **rag paper** |
| **Fish** | The **Humboldt Current** off the coast (anchovy, sardines) | Food, fish meal, fertilizer |
| **Quinine bark (cinchona), rubber, coca** | Eastern slopes and the Amazon edge | Medicine, rubber seals and belts |

| Phase | Years | Key tasks and inventions | Why now, here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0–2** | 1230–32 | **Quarantine**, boiled water, **oral rehydration**, **soap**, latrines away from water, clean childbirth. **Raised fields** revived. Better chuño. **The wheelbarrow** (a simple thing nobody here has: one wheel, two handles, triples what one person moves) | Costs nothing but knowledge. Wins mothers, then the ayllu, then the lord |
| **3–5** | 1233–35 | **Standard measures**, census quipus, **storehouse design** (ventilated, cold, dry), **Arabic-style written numerals** alongside the quipu. **Pulleys and the crank**. **Tin bronze** standardized. Simple **pit kilns for lime** | The storehouse network *is* his power, so it must be counted and trusted |
| **6–10** | 1236–40 | **Graded roads** (ramps, not stairs, so wheels work), **llama carts** (light two-wheeled carts; a llama can pull more than it can carry, *reasoned*), better rope bridges. **A syllable script** for Aymara and Quechua, written on cloth and bark. **Quipu schools**. Guano trade with the coast starts | Writing lets orders and lessons travel without him. Roads let food reach a starving valley in days |
| **11–20** | 1241–50 | **Water wheels** on highland streams (grinding, bellows, saws). **Bronze cast in molds** for standard tools and pump parts. **Clear glass** from sand and plant ash, then **spectacles** and lenses. **Rag paper** from cotton. **Seismic building rules**. A **fish-drying and salting** industry with coast partners | Water power replaces the missing draft animals. Paper makes the schools cheap |
| **21–50** | 1251–80 | **Printing** with **cast bronze type** (bronze is plentiful, and a syllable script needs few characters). **Iron**: a first smelter in the **Santa valley**, using anthracite and ore brought by sea, with the Chimú as partners. **Water-powered bellows**. **Sailing rafts with centerboards** along the coast (the coast people already use centerboards, called *guaras*). **Microscope**, then **germ theory** taught in schools. **Lime mortar and concrete**. Blasting powder from Atacama nitrates for road tunnels | Iron needs coal, and coal is on the coast. That means the coast must be a partner first, so iron waits until about 1260 |
| **51–100** | 1281–1330 | **Iron rails and wagonways** on the steepest routes. **A Newcomen-style steam pump** for mines and lifting water (anthracite fuel). **Iron ploughs pulled by people or water-powered winches**. **Crucible steel**. **Superphosphate** from guano and acid. **Electricity basics**: batteries from copper and zinc, electromagnets, then a **telegraph** along the road network. Vulcanized **rubber** belts and seals | By now there are millions of literate people, coal and iron on the coast, and decades of trained engineers. The EE student's own field finally pays off: **a telegraph turns the runner post into instant feedback** |

**The simple things with huge effects here:**
- **The wheel on a graded road.** The Andes knew the wheel (as toys) but had no animal to pull a cart on mountain stairs. **Ramps plus wheelbarrows plus llama carts** change everything.
- **Raised fields.** Already invented, then abandoned. Reviving them could feed hundreds of thousands at the lake.
- **Writing the quipu.** The quipu already has place value. He only has to add **sounds**.

## Food, water and health
**First tasks, in order:**
1. **Quarantine himself on arrival** (about 3 weeks). Then, for his first year, he stays away from anyone who is sick, washes often, and teaches **boiling water and handwashing**.
2. **Oral rehydration** for child diarrhea (clean water, a pinch of salt, a handful of honey or sweet maize syrup).
3. **Soap** (fat plus ash lye) and **latrines** downhill from water.
4. **Clean childbirth**: boiled cloths, washed hands, trained midwives in every ayllu.
5. **Raised fields** and **terrace repair** for frost and drought safety.
6. **Chuño and dried-meat (charqui) reserves**, then **dried fish** from the coast, in storehouses on every road.
7. **Guano on fields.** It can double or triple yields (*the Inca guarded the guano birds by law, and earlier coast peoples already used it*).
8. **Diet gaps:** iodine (goiter is common in the highlands), fixed by **seaweed and coast salt** sent up the roads. Vitamin C from fresh potatoes and fruit from the warm valleys.

**The disease problem (the biggest single danger in this variation):**
- **Him, on arrival.** He's a modern person, probably vaccinated against smallpox-era killers like measles, and **smallpox no longer exists**, so he likely doesn't carry the great killers. But he may carry **cold and flu viruses, stomach bugs and other germs** these people have never met (*reasoned, not measured*). The quarantine is cheap insurance.
- **First contact, later.** When Old World ships arrive (1492 in real history, or earlier if his people sail out), **smallpox, measles, typhus and flu could kill up to 90%**. Normal vaccination needs **cowpox**, and **there are no cattle in the Americas**.
- **His options, as the centuries pass:**
  - **A quarantine law at every port**: 40 days offshore for any foreign ship. Writing and the runner post make this enforceable.
  - **Germ theory in every school**, so quarantine is understood, not resented.
  - **A planned voyage out** (decades ahead), across the Pacific to Asia or around to Europe, to bring back **cowpox arm-to-arm** in a chain of volunteers (Spain did this with orphans in 1803), or to bring **cattle** themselves. That probably belongs to his successors, around 1350–1450.

## Trade and the outside world
**Inside the Andes (the real economy):** the old **"vertical archipelago"**. The highlands send **wool, chuño, meat and bronze** down; the coast sends **cotton, fish, salt, guano and maize** up; the eastern valleys send **coca, fruit, timber and medicines**. He **speeds it up** with roads, carts, writing and storehouses.

**The coast partnership (the key deal):** the **Chimú** are a real state with a king, irrigation and fine crafts. He doesn't conquer them. He offers:
- **Highland water security.** Coast rivers start in the mountains. Joint canal and reservoir work.
- **El Niño insurance.** When El Niño floods the coast and kills the fishery, the highland storehouses feed Chan Chan.
- **Bronze tools, wool and quipu-writing**, in exchange for **coal, iron ore, guano, fish and sea transport**.
- Over decades, a **federation** with the Chimú king keeping his throne and court.

**The outside world:** none, in practice, without ocean ships. Possible far contacts: **Ecuador and Mexico** by coastal raft (Mexico has millions of people, maize, rubber and writing), and **Polynesia** (DNA suggests Polynesians and South Americans met around **1150–1230**; *a 2020 study, debated*).

**Secrets:** with no outside rival, secrecy hardly matters. **Knowledge spreads freely inside the network**; that's the point. The one thing he keeps quiet is **the coming of the Europeans**, shared only with his successors.

## Story texture
**Tensions:**
- **The steward becomes the state.** He starts by counting potatoes; the counting **turns into power** without him ever taking a crown.
- **Liars in the quipus.** A kuraka fakes his harvest knots to look good. The **double-count** catches it, or doesn't, and a valley goes hungry.
- **Old ways vs. new ways.** A grandmother who knows a hundred potato varieties teaches him more than he teaches her. Some of his "improvements" fail.
- **Coast and mountains.** Chan Chan is richer and prouder than anything at the lake.
- **The secret of 1532.** How do you prepare a continent for a danger 300 years away without frightening it?
- **Disease guilt.** Every cough in his first year terrifies him.

**Real people and places he might meet (dates uncertain):**
- The **early Inca kings** of Cusco, a small, ambitious family in his time. A young prince of Cusco could become his student. In real history, **Pachacuti** (who built the empire) comes much later (reigned c. 1438–1471).
- The **Chimú dynasty** at Chan Chan. The founder **Taycanamo** and later kings like **Ñancenpinco** (an expander) are known from legends; *who sits on the throne in the 1250s is uncertain*.
- **Lupaca and Colla lords** (the later Lupaca **Cari** family is known by name; the 1230 lords are not, so the story invents them).

**Scenes:**
- **The hut on the hill:** three weeks alone, watching the first village from a distance, rehearsing Aymara words.
- **The night of the frost:** the raised fields steam in the dark while every other field on the shore turns black.
- **The first double-count:** two quipus that don't match, and the inspector who walks three days to find out why.
- **El Niño:** the sea turns warm, the fish vanish, Chan Chan floods, and llama trains loaded with chuño come down the new road.
- **The first iron:** black stones from the Santa valley, a water-driven bellows, and a glowing bloom no Andean has ever seen.
- **The succession test:** he goes away for a year on purpose, and the council runs everything without him.
