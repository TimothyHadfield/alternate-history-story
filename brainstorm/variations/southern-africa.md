# Southern Africa: the Rainmaker's Kingdom

*A lone stranger makes a sacred king drought-proof, then walks the kingdom up onto the healthy highveld, next to coal, iron and salt, and lets the world in through one gate.*

**This is one variation for comparison, not a decision.** Nothing here is chosen.

## What makes this one different
- **He arrives alone, with nothing, in a land with no writing.** No court to climb, no scholars to meet, no books to borrow. Everything starts with learning a language from scratch and proving himself with his hands.
- **He partners with an existing sacred king instead of founding or ruling.** The king's power rests on **bringing rain**. In this variation he never competes with that. He makes the king **look like the rain-bringer even in drought** by storing water: wells, dams and irrigation become **the king's gifts**.
- **The kingdom moves.** It starts in the hot, malarial Limpopo valley and, over ~40 years, shifts its core **450 km south onto the cool highveld plateau**, next to **Witbank coal, Thabazimbi iron and Tswaing salt and soda**. History already abandoned Mapungubwe around 1290. Here the move happens on purpose, early, and without a collapse.
- **Growth comes mostly from neighbors walking in.** A kingdom of ~9,000 becomes hundreds of thousands by drawing in nearby farming and herding peoples who share a cattle culture, not by refugee fleets or conquest.
- **One trade gate: Sofala.** Gold and ivory go out. **Horses, wheat, donkeys, paper and craftsmen** come in by Indian Ocean dhow. No outsider is allowed inland.

## The setup
| | |
|---|---|
| **Arrival** | **1232**, on the Limpopo, near the Shashe confluence (today's border of South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe) |
| **The kingdom** | **Mapungubwe**, at its height. ~**9,000 people** in the kingdom, ~**3,000–5,000** around the capital. The elite moved up onto **Mapungubwe Hill around 1220** during a dry spell; the hill became the kingdom's **rainmaking site**, and the king became a **sacred, hidden ruler** above the commoners below |
| **Wealth** | **Cattle** (wealth, marriage payments, status), sorghum, millet, cowpeas, ivory, and **gold** traded down to the coast. Famous find: the small **golden rhinoceros** from the royal graves |
| **Outside contact** | Glass beads and cloth from India and the Middle East, a little Chinese celadon, all through **Sofala** and the Swahili city of **Kilwa** |
| **Skills already there** | Iron smelting, copper and gold working, cotton spinning, pottery, terraced fields, ivory carving |
| **Missing** | **Writing, the wheel, the plough, horses, draft animals in harness, wheat, stone masonry with mortar** |
| **Language** | Probably an early form of **Shona** or a close relative (*uncertain*; archaeologists link Mapungubwe's people to the later Great Zimbabwe culture). Nobody speaks anything he knows. **Arabic and Swahili are only spoken at the coast** |
| **What he has** | His body, his clothes, and an EE student's head: physics, chemistry basics, how electricity and engines work, hygiene, medicine basics, farming ideas, maths |

**The world around him:** the Mongols are 8,000 km away and will never come. No ship rounds the Cape until **1488**. Europeans reach this plateau in the **1830s**. He has **~250 years** before anyone outside could even try to interfere.

## The rise to power
The route: **useful stranger → healer of people and cattle → water-maker for the king → royal in-law → the king's right hand.**

| Years | Step | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **1232–1234** | **Survive and learn** | He is a strange-looking man with no cattle and no kin, so he has **no status at all**. Most likely he's taken in as a dependent of a commoner homestead below the hill. Two years of herding, hoeing, and learning the language like a child. He notices everything: where water sits, how cattle die, why children die |
| **1233** | **First proof: the child** | A child dying of diarrhea is saved with **oral rehydration**: thin sorghum gruel with a pinch of salt, sip after sip. It looks like nothing. It works overnight. Mothers talk |
| **1234** | **Second proof: the well in the dry season** | When the river has shrunk to sand, he digs a **lined well** at a spot he picked by reading the land (low ground, green trees, the inside of a river bend). It gives water all winter |
| **1234–1236** | **Cattle doctor** | He keeps a sick herd alive: **separate the sick animals**, **burn the carcasses** of ones that die suddenly (anthrax), give **salt licks**, and cut and store **hay** for the dry months so cattle don't starve in late winter. **Cattle are wealth, so saving cattle is saving fortunes.** The cattle-owning elite notice |
| **1236** | **Summoned up the hill** | The king's people want the stranger who keeps herds alive. Here is the **key risk**: rainmaking belongs to the king and his ritual experts. A stranger who "makes water" could look like a rival. **His answer: every well, every dam is dug in the king's name, opened in the king's ceremony.** He makes himself the king's tool, never the king's rival |
| **1237–1239** | **The king's water** | A chain of **wells and small earth dams** around the capital. In a dry year, the kingdom still has water, and the people credit the **king**. This is the core insight of the variation: **the rainmaker king's greatest fear is a drought that proves his power failed. The stranger removes that fear.** |
| **~1238** | **Marriage** | He marries into the royal family (a daughter or niece of the king; the details are Tim's call). Marriage turns him from an outsider into **kin**, which in this society is the only real form of belonging |
| **1240** | **Real power** | He holds a new office, roughly **"keeper of the king's water and cattle"**: control over water works, herds' health, the new crafts, and soon the **coast trade**. The king stays sacred and above. He runs the work below |

**The one secret proof (optional):** if he ever tells anyone the truth, it's the king, once, in private. He could describe the **gold rhinoceros in the king's grandfather's grave** or say what the coast traders will ask for next season. The story works without it, since water and cattle are proof enough.

**Risks along the way:** being killed or enslaved as a stranger in year one; the ritual experts turning on him; a bad drought before the dams exist; the king dying and an heir who doesn't trust him (which is why marriage matters).

## How the group grows
**Population sources, in order of size:**
| Source | Who | Why they come |
|---|---|---|
| **Neighbors joining** (the biggest) | Nearby farming peoples of the Limpopo and Zimbabwe plateau; from **~1300**, **Sotho-Tswana** groups arriving on the highveld (archaeologists tie them to *Moloko* pottery) | Water in drought, cattle medicine, iron hoes and ploughs, food stores, no famine. People in this culture **already move to strong, prosperous chiefs**. Mapungubwe's own people later moved to Great Zimbabwe for the same reason |
| **Births** | Everyone | Clean water, ORS, soap and midwives with washed hands can **roughly halve child deaths** |
| **Herders** | **Khoisan** herders and hunters to the west and south | Trade, cattle, trackers, later horse riders |
| **The coast** | Swahili craftsmen; Gujarati weavers and shipwrights; **captives bought free** from the Indian Ocean slave trade and given land | Pay, land, freedom |

**How they're absorbed:** a joining chief keeps his people, his title and his land, and gets **wells, a granary, iron tools and a teacher**. In return he sends **young men and women for service** (a few years on dams, mines and roads, learning skills) and joins the king's council. The joining peoples speak related **Bantu languages**, so learning the shared tongue is quick.

**Target numbers** (aiming at maximum births plus maximum joining, while food per person never falls):

| Year | People | Mostly from |
|---|---|---|
| 1232 | ~9,000 | The kingdom |
| 1250 | ~25,000 | Nearby homesteads joining |
| 1270 | ~80,000 | Whole neighboring chiefdoms |
| 1300 | ~250,000 | The highveld and the Zimbabwe plateau |
| 1330 | ~600,000 | Births at ~3% a year plus joiners |
| 1400 | ~2 million | Mostly births |

**Keeping up:** every new group gets **water before people** (dam and well first, then huts). Granaries hold **two years of grain**. Housing moves from mud-and-pole to **fired brick with lime mortar** once kilns exist.

## Government and society
- **The sacred king stays.** He remains the ritual center, the rain-bringer, hidden on the hill (later on a new sacred hill on the highveld). The hero is his **in-law and chief minister**, not a rival king.
- **The local culture already has the tools:** a **council of headmen**, **cattle loans** (lending cattle to clients binds people to you), **age-sets** (young people initiated together, ready to work as a group), and **cattle posts** (herds kept far away on good grazing). He uses all of them:
  - **Age-set service** becomes **national service**: each initiation group spends two to three years on the dams, the iron works and the roads.
  - **Cattle loans** become **a bank**: the state lends breeding cattle and ploughs, repaid in calves and grain.
  - **Cattle posts** become **the move**: the first highveld settlements are royal cattle posts, a normal thing to found.
- **Writing:** he makes a **simple alphabet for the local language** (Latin letters, one sound per letter, no silent spellings). First taught to the **king's children**, then the sons and daughters of every joining chief. Writing is for **cattle records, grain stores, water levels and births**. This is the variation's quiet superweapon: **a whole state learning to read in its own tongue at once, with no old scribal class to resist.**
- **Schools:** one in every town by 1270; reading, numbers, hygiene, cattle care, then trades. Girls too, since women farm and run households.
- **Feedback:** the **council of headmen** is where bad news is reported, and headmen are rewarded for reporting drought and sickness early, never punished for it.
- **Succession:** he plans for it from the start. **His children by the royal marriage** are raised as both royal kin and trained administrators. A **written council** (the king, the head of the water works, the head of schools, elected headmen) is set up to run things if he dies, and is **tested by letting it run for a season while he is away** at the coast.

## The tech path
**Why this order, here:** water first, because rain decides everything and the king's standing rests on it. Then **cattle and food**. Then **the move** to the highveld, because valley disease and drought cap the population. Only then **coal and iron at scale**, because the highveld has little wood and the coal is right there.

**The resources and where they are:**
| Resource | Place | Distance (rough) |
|---|---|---|
| Copper | **Musina** | ~70 km from Mapungubwe |
| Copper and iron, worked since ~700 AD | **Phalaborwa** | ~250 km east |
| Coal | Limpopo/Tuli fields; the **Waterberg** field | ~50–200 km |
| **Iron ore** | **Thabazimbi** ("mountain of iron") | ~150 km from the highveld core |
| **Tin** | **Rooiberg** | ~120 km from the core |
| **Coal, shallow, huge** | **Witbank** | ~110 km east of the core |
| **Salt and soda ash** | **Tswaing** crater ("place of salt") | ~40 km north of the core |
| **Reliable springs, limestone (dolomite)** | Around later **Pretoria** | The core itself |
| **Shipbuilding timber** | **Knysna** forests | Far south coast, later |

The highveld core sits at the **dolomite springs where Pretoria later grew**: water that doesn't fail in drought, lime for mortar and fertilizer, and **salt, iron, tin and coal all within ~150 km**.

| Phase | Key tasks and inventions | Why now, here |
|---|---|---|
| **Years 0–2** (1232–34) | Language. **ORS**, boiling water, **soap** (cattle fat + wood-ash lye), latrines away from water. **Lined wells**. Hay and salt licks for cattle, quarantine of sick animals. A private notebook in his own head: numbers, a script | He has nothing but his hands. Everything must be **free, local and visible within days** |
| **Years 3–5** (1235–37) | **Earth dams and check dams** in the dry streams, **sand-river wells**, rainwater tanks. **The ox-plough** (iron share from local smiths, wooden frame). **Compost and manure** on fields, **cowpeas rotated** with sorghum. Sealed **granaries** (raised, plastered, ash against weevils). Better bellows and taller **furnaces**. **Hindu-Arabic numerals** and simple accounting | The kingdom's weakness is water and the length of the dry season. The plough is the biggest single jump: **one ox team does the work of ~10 hoes**, and cattle are already here |
| **Years 6–10** (1238–42) | **The wheel**: ox-carts and wheelbarrows. Unknown here, and the flat plateau is perfect for it. **Persian water-wheels** (*saqiya*, ox-driven, a chain of pots) for irrigation. **Kiln-fired bricks** and **lime mortar**. **The alphabet** and first school. **Spinning wheel** and better loom for local cotton. Standard **weights and measures**. First **royal cattle posts on the highveld** at the springs | The wheel and the cart are the "simple things nobody thought of" here, and they're needed **before** the move: 450 km of trade and goods can't go on heads |
| **Years 11–20** (1243–52) | **Tswaing salt and soda works** (soda for **glass** and **soap**). **Glass**: bottles, then **lenses** and spectacles. **Coal from the Witbank outcrops**, first for lime kilns and smithing, then **coke**. A **coke blast furnace** for **cast iron**: ploughs, pots, pipes. **Paper** from grass and cotton rags, and a **wooden press with movable type** (the short alphabet makes this easy). **Horses, donkeys and wheat** arrive from Sofala. **Highveld core becomes a town**. **Smallpox prevention** with cowpox, *if* cowpox can be found in the herds (*uncertain*) | The highveld has almost **no wood**, so **coal comes early, out of need**. Tswaing's **natural soda** gives glass and soap without any chemistry. Printing matters more than anywhere else, because there is **no handwritten book culture to replace** |
| **Years 21–50** (1253–82) | **The court moves to the highveld** (finished before the late-1200s drought). **Wagon roads**: highveld → Limpopo → Sofala. **Wooden-railed wagonways** at the mines. **Pumping engines** (Newcomen-style atmospheric steam) to drain the iron and coal pits. **Crucible steel**, steel tools. **Sulfuric acid** from pyrite, **fertilizer** from bone and lime. **Water-wheels and windmills** where the Magaliesberg streams allow. A **coastal port at Delagoa Bay** (Maputo) and a shipyard; first **own sea-going ships** with compass, sternpost rudder and frame-built hulls. **A voyage to the Americas** (around the Cape, then west) for **maize, potatoes and cassava**, which suit this land perfectly. **Telescopes**, a printed **almanac** of rains | Coal and iron are in place; now **energy and transport** limit growth. **Maize is the single biggest food gain for this land**, but it's an ocean away, so the ships come before the farms can jump |
| **Years 51–100** (1283–1332) | **Steam locomotives on iron rails** across the plateau. Steam-powered **cotton and iron mills**. **Batteries** (copper from Musina and Phalaborwa), **telegraph** lines along the railway. **A university** at the core. A **wheat-and-grape settlement at the Cape**, and **Knysna shipyards**. Deep shaft mining. **Wells drilled** with steam rigs. Surveys of what lies beyond (Australia via the Roaring Forties is a far option) | By now there are hundreds of thousands of literate people. **His knowledge stops being the limit; their own inventors take over** |

## Food, water and health
**The first tasks, in order:**
1. **ORS** for every mother who'll listen (gruel + salt). The cheapest life-saver there is.
2. **Boil or settle drinking water**; latrines dug **downhill and away** from wells.
3. **Soap** from cattle fat and ash lye; **washed hands for midwives**.
4. **Lined wells** at every homestead cluster, then **earth dams** on the dry streams to catch summer storms.
5. **Hay for winter**: cut grass at the end of the rains, stack it, and the herds survive late winter instead of starving.
6. **Salt licks** for cattle (Soutpansberg salt, later Tswaing).
7. **Sealed granaries** holding **two years** of grain: quietly ready for the dry years he knows the region suffers.
8. **Move off the valley floor**: people sleep on higher ground, cotton **mosquito nets** over sleeping mats, and herds kept out of the **tsetse** bush.
9. **Ox-ploughing and manure**: more food from the same field.
10. **Winter wheat under irrigation** on the highveld once seed arrives: the highveld's dry winter is useless for local crops, so **wheat on dam water is a second harvest nobody here could have**.

**Health on the highveld:** frost kills mosquitoes and tsetse. **Moving the core uphill is itself the biggest health measure** in this variation.

## Trade and the outside world
**The gate is Sofala** (near today's Beira, Mozambique), the southern end of the monsoon trade. Arab, Persian, Swahili and Gujarati dhows reach it; currents and storms stop them going further south. **Kilwa**'s sultans control most of the Sofala gold trade.

| Goes out | Comes in |
|---|---|
| **Gold** (what the buyers want; he doesn't value it much himself) | **Horses** and **donkeys** (by dhow from Arabia and India) |
| **Ivory** | **Wheat**, rice, sugarcane, citrus seed |
| Copper ingots | **Paper** and books in Arabic (for maths and astronomy), glass beads less and less |
| Later: cotton cloth, iron pots, glass | **Craftsmen**: shipwrights, weavers, a glassmaker or two, for pay and land |
| | Cloth at first, then less as local mills grow |

- **Horses are the tricky import.** **African horse sickness** (carried by midges) kills them in the warm lowlands. Horses landed at Sofala walk inland **in the dry winter**, fast, straight up to the frosty highveld, where they can survive. Many die on the first tries. **Donkeys are tougher** and come first.
- **Only one gate, and outsiders stay at it.** Foreign traders deal at a **fixed market town on the trade road**, never on the plateau. This copies what the region already did: coast traders seldom went inland.
- **What's kept secret:** **how** things are made. Finished goods (cloth, iron pots) may go out; looms, furnaces, the plough, the alphabet and printing don't. The alphabet itself helps: **coast traders can't read it**.
- **What's allowed to spread:** ORS, soap, wells and hay are given freely to any neighbor. **Health that spreads brings joiners, not rivals.**

## Story texture
**The core tensions:**
- **The rainmaker and the engineer.** The king's power is that he brings rain. The hero can't make rain, only store it. Their whole partnership is a quiet agreement about **who gets credit**, and it has to hold for decades.
- **Leaving the sacred hill.** Mapungubwe Hill is where the kings are buried and where rain is made. Moving the court 450 km south means building **a new sacred hill**, and convincing the ritual experts that the ancestors approve.
- **Writing a language that never had letters.** Which sounds get which letters? What do they call the alphabet? The first letter written home from the coast.
- **Gold versus iron.** The outside world only wants gold. The hero only wants iron and coal. Everyone thinks he's mad for valuing black rocks.
- **The horses that keep dying.** Year after year the dhows bring horses and most die on the walk inland, until someone works out the winter road.

**Real people and places he could meet:**
- The **king of Mapungubwe** (his name isn't known, so Tim can name him), the queen mother, the ritual specialists.
- The sultans of **Kilwa**: the **Mahdali** dynasty from ~1277, and later **al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman**, who built the great palace **Husuni Kubwa** (*dates approximate*).
- **Ibn Battuta**, passing through Kilwa in **1331**.
- A Gujarati shipwright, a Swahili trader at Sofala, a Khoisan tracker who becomes the first horseman, a Sotho-Tswana chief who brings his whole people in.

**Scenes:**
- A child saved with salt and gruel, in a language he can't speak yet.
- The first well that still has water when the river is sand.
- The king opening "his" dam in a ceremony the hero designed.
- The first wheel turning on the highveld grass, and people walking alongside to watch it.
- **The first coal fire at Witbank**: black stone that burns.
- The royal daughter (his wife? his child?) reading the first printed page aloud.
- The real-history drought of the 1290s rolling in, and the granaries opening.
- A Kilwa trader asking where the cloth is woven, and getting no answer.

| With the Cape and Australia as later sites | **9** in the earlier homeland rating |

**Strengths**
- **~250 years of guaranteed peace** from outside. Ideas stay home for generations.
- **Coal, iron, tin, copper, salt and soda all on one plateau**, close together.
- **Cattle everywhere**: oxen for ploughs and carts from day one.
- **No old elite of scribes, guilds or nobles** to resist new ways. A whole nation learns to read at once.
- A ready-made **trade gate** at Sofala, with no need to build ships early.
- **Room to grow**: the plateau, the Zimbabwe plateau, the Cape.

**Weaknesses**
- **The hardest start of all six**: alone, no language, no status.
- **Small population** (~9,000), so growth depends on neighbors choosing to join.
- **Unreliable rain** and **no navigable rivers**: everything moves by ox-cart until rail.
- **Horses and wheat must be imported**, and **maize and potatoes are an ocean away**.
- **Little timber** on the plateau.
- **Everything hangs on one partnership** with one sacred king, and then his heir.

## Open questions
1. **Marriage:** is a royal marriage part of the story, and how central?
2. **The one secret:** does he ever tell the king the truth, or is water proof enough?
3. **The move:** does the whole court move by ~1280, or does Mapungubwe stay as a second capital until the drought?
4. **Names:** the king, the alphabet, and the new highveld capital at the springs.
5. **The Americas voyage:** worth the decades of ship-building for maize and potatoes, or skip it?
6. **Great Zimbabwe:** joined in as a partner, or left as a separate neighbor?
