## The ten extra factors
Each scored 0–10, higher is better:

| Factor | What it means for a 1230s society |
|---|---|
| **Water** | Reliable rain and rivers. Without water there are no crops, cattle, cities or water mills |
| **Climate** | Length of the growing season, frost, and whether the main crops grow well |
| **Disasters** | Droughts, floods, earthquakes, volcanoes, storms, famines (**10 = safest**) |
| **Food** | How much food the land can grow and fish, with local crops and animals |
| **Import access** | How easily missing crops, animals and strategic materials can be brought in |
| **Health** | Disease burden: malaria, sleeping sickness, plague (**10 = healthiest**) |
| **Energy** | Water power, wind, firewood and coal to run mills and later engines |
| **Transport** | Rivers, coast and terrain. Moving iron and grain overland costs 10–50× more than by water |
| **Building** | Stone, clay, lime and timber for cities, dams, kilns and furnaces |
| **Room to grow** | Empty or thinly settled land to expand into |

**The big shift:** **southern Africa drops from first to fourth.** Its land is hard: **unreliable rain, droughts, malaria and tsetse in the valleys, no navigable rivers.** Its minerals are great, but the land around them is tough to live and move on. **North America and Britain** have the easiest land to develop on: water everywhere, rivers to ship on, water power, healthy climates.

## Place by place
### North America (Ohio Valley, Great Lakes)
- **Water and energy:** the Great Lakes and the Ohio–Mississippi system are some of the best fresh water and **water-power sites on Earth**, plus coal and endless firewood.
- **Climate:** cold snowy winters and hot summers; a 150–180 day growing season, good for maize, wheat, oats.
- **Disasters:** river floods, tornadoes, hard winters. **Droughts in the 1200s**: the Southwest's "Great Drought" (1276–1299), and Cahokia, the biggest city north of Mexico, was declining. The New Madrid earthquakes are rare.
- **Food:** the best soils on the list. Local: maize, beans, squash, sunflower, plus deer, bison, fish and wild rice. **No farm animals except dogs and turkeys.**
- **Imports (the weak point):** every Old World crop and animal (wheat, rice, cattle, horses, pigs, sheep, chickens) needs an **Atlantic crossing**.
- **Health:** healthy and temperate. **But newcomers bring smallpox and measles** that could kill most Native people. The hero could prevent this with **cowpox vaccination** using cattle brought over.
- **Transport:** the best on Earth: rivers go everywhere.

### Britain
- **Water and energy:** reliable rain and rivers. England had about **6,000 water mills by 1086**; there's wind and coal too.
- **Climate:** cool and wet; wheat, barley, oats, grazing.
- **Disasters:** the **cold, wet summer of 1258** (after a huge volcanic eruption in Indonesia) brought famine. The **Great Famine of 1315–1317** came with the cooling known as the Little Ice Age. Floods and storms; no earthquakes.
- **Food:** grain, sheep, cattle, herring and cod.
- **Imports:** short sea trips to all of Europe.
- **Health:** the **Black Death (1348–49)** killed ~40%. Otherwise moderate.
- **Room to grow:** the weak point. A small, full island, so growth means ships.

### Sweden
- **Water and energy:** lakes and rivers everywhere, endless wood and water power.
- **Climate (the weak point):** a short growing season. The **Little Ice Age (from ~1300) hits hardest in the north**. Barley, rye, oats.
- **Food:** thin farming, but cattle and lots of Baltic herring.
- **Salt has to be imported** (needed for preserving fish and meat).
- **Transport:** lakes, coast, and **sledges over frozen ground in winter**.

### Java (+ Australia)
- **Food (the strength):** volcanic soils and monsoon rains give **2–3 rice harvests a year**. Among the richest farmland on Earth.
- **Disasters (the weakness):** volcanoes, earthquakes, tsunamis. **Samalas, on Lombok just east of Java and Bali, erupted in 1257 in what may be the biggest eruption of the last 7,000 years.** Tambora (1815) is nearby too.
- **Health:** malaria, dengue and other tropical diseases.
- **Imports:** the Asian trade hub, so all Asian crops and animals are within reach.
- **Australia's half** adds iron, coal, room and healthy land, but **poor water and droughts**.

### Japan
- **Disasters (the weakness):** earthquakes, tsunamis, typhoons and volcanoes. The **Kangi famine (1230–31)**, from cold weather, was one of Japan's worst.
- **Food:** rice and fish, but only ~15% of the land is flat enough to farm.
- **Energy:** short, steep rivers, which are great for water wheels.
- **Transport:** the **Seto Inland Sea** is a sheltered highway.
- **Imports:** China and Korea are close.

### Southern Africa
- **Where to put the core matters.** **Mapungubwe sits in the hot Limpopo valley**: malaria, **tsetse fly** (sleeping sickness, which also kills cattle and horses), and ~350–400 mm of rain a year. **The highveld plateau** to the south (~1,500 m up, around later Pretoria and Johannesburg) is **cooler, healthier and wetter (600–800 mm)**. Frosty winters there kill pests, and it is **next to the big shallow coalfields (Witbank) and iron**. So a strong option is **start at Mapungubwe, build the core on the highveld**.
- **Water (the weak point):** summer rain that fails in drought years. Rivers shrink to sand in winter. **Dams, wells and irrigation would be the hero's first big gift.**
- **Disasters:** frequent droughts, locusts. **A drought in the early 1300s is linked to Mapungubwe's abandonment** (scholars debate how much climate versus politics caused it). No earthquakes or volcanoes.
- **Food:** sorghum, millet, cowpeas and cattle; modest yields. **Maize (from the Americas) later became southern Africa's staple: the climate suits it perfectly**, but getting it means reaching the Americas first.
- **Imports:** through Sofala, from the Indian Ocean world: rice, sugarcane, Asian crops, **horses by dhow from Arabia or India** (they survive on the highveld, but African horse sickness kills them in the lowlands), and wheat for winter growing.
- **Energy:** few strong rivers and little wood on the plateau, so **coal and steam are needed early**.
- **Transport:** no navigable rivers. **Ox-wagons on the flat plateau**, later rail.
- **Room to grow:** huge, from the plateau to Zimbabwe to the Cape.

### Central Chile
- **Climate (the strength):** Mediterranean, like California: wheat, grapes, fruit, with Andean snowmelt for irrigation.
- **Disasters (the weakness):** **the strongest earthquakes on Earth** (1960, magnitude 9.5), tsunamis, volcanoes.
- **Food:** potatoes, maize, quinoa, and the **Humboldt Current**, the richest fishing waters in the world.
- **Imports:** almost none reachable.

### Ethiopia
- **Climate:** highland "eternal spring", with teff, wheat and barley. **Coffee grows wild here.**
- **Disasters:** droughts and famines when the rains fail; locusts.
- **Energy and transport (the weakness):** the forests were already thin, and deep gorges make moving anything hard. No navigable rivers.
- **Salt** comes from the Danakil desert as bars, which were even used as money.

### Andes
- **Food (the strength):** **potatoes** (thousands of kinds), quinoa, maize, **freeze-dried potatoes (chuño) that keep for years**, the Humboldt fisheries and **guano fertilizer**.
- **Disasters:** earthquakes and **El Niño years** that flood the coast and dry the highlands.
- **Water:** the coast is desert, watered only by rivers from the mountains.
- **Transport:** the worst on the list.

### Southeast Australia
- **Health:** the healthiest on the list.
- **Disasters:** droughts, bushfires, floods.
- **Food:** **nothing to farm at first** (no native crops or farm animals). With imported wheat and sheep, it later became one of the world's great food exporters.
- **Imports:** the farthest from everything. The nearest crops are in New Guinea and Indonesia.

**What it means:** knowing these years lets the hero **quietly store grain and prepare** (no public prophecy), and it makes Europe's 1300s much harder than its 1200s.

### Crops and animals that change everything if moved
- **From the Americas to everywhere else:** **potato** (it later doubled Europe's food supply), **maize** (the future staple of southern Africa), **cassava**, **sweet potato**, beans, tomatoes. Any non-American site needs **an Atlantic or Pacific voyage** to get them.
- **From the Old World to the Americas:** wheat, rice, **cattle, horses, oxen, pigs, sheep, chickens**, iron tools.
- **Across Asia and Africa:** rice, sugarcane, bananas, citrus, horses and camels are reachable through Indian Ocean trade.

### Is Britain too close to powerful neighbors? (Tim, 2026-10-28)
"It seems like britain could be scary with it so close to so many powerful civilizations?"

**Invasion from outside is a smaller worry than it looks. Being inside a crowded Europe is the real one.**
- **The sea protects it.** England was last conquered in 1066. Since then, invasions that took over or toppled the government were rare: **Prince Louis of France held London in 1216–17** (invited in by rebel barons) and William of Orange arrived in 1688 (also invited). The Armada (1588), Napoleon and Hitler all failed.
- **The Mongols never came close.** They stopped in Hungary and Poland in 1241–42. The only effect on England recorded at the time: **Baltic traders stayed home, so herring went unsold** (Matthew Paris).
- **The real dangers are close by and political:**
  - **The king and barons:** the hero isn't the ruler, and civil wars came in 1215–17 and 1264–65.
  - **The Pope:** England had been a papal fief since 1213, and the Church could excommunicate people or put the country under interdict, as it did in 1208–14.
  - **Copying:** France, Flanders and Italy are days away, so any new weapon, ship or method **spreads to rivals within years** and starts an arms race. In southern Africa or North America, an idea stays home for generations.
  - **Wars of choice:** England's kings fight Wales (conquered 1282–83), Scotland (from 1296) and France (the Hundred Years' War, from 1337).
- **The same closeness is also Britain's biggest strength:** scholars, books, trade, imports and skilled settlers are all nearby.

**Neighbor danger:** about **5/10** for Britain (10 = safest). Compare southern Africa ~8 and North America ~9 (no rival with ships or steel until ~1500), and Japan ~6 (the Mongol fleets of 1274 and 1281).

## How easy is it to rise to power there? (Tim, 2026-10-28)

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **North America with settlers** | — | — | **8** | **8** | He **founds his own state**, so there's no one to climb past. Local chiefdoms are small and the tech gap is the largest |
| **North America**, alone | 5 | 4 | 8 | **5** | Small chiefdoms and a huge tech gap, so becoming a chief through prestige or marriage is realistic. But **no one ruler controls the region**, so uniting it is slow |
| **Java** | 6 | 4 | 5 | **5** | Foreigners are normal at court. **Ken Arok, a commoner and outlaw, made himself king and founded Singhasari in 1222**, just before the story starts. Rivals plot constantly |
| **Andes** | 4 | 4 | 6 | **5** | **No empire yet in 1230**: small kingdoms (Cusco is a minor one) plus the Chimú state on the coast. Fragmented, so there's room to rise. No writing, and it's a long way from everything |
| **Southern Africa** | 4 | 3 | 6 | **4** | **A small elite around one sacred king**, so the right route (water, healing, cattle, marriage) can win it all. But the language barrier is total, and a stranger can threaten the king's rainmaking role |
| **Britain** | 5 | 1 | 1 | **3** | Power comes from birth and land. **Henry III promoted foreigners**: Simon de Montfort, a Frenchman, married the king's sister in 1238 and **effectively ruled England in 1264–65**. So adviser or in-law is possible, but holding power is dangerous, and Montfort was killed at Evesham |
| **Sweden** | 4 | 2 | 3 | **3** | The magnates pick the king. **Birger Jarl ruled as regent from 1248 to 1266**, so a strong man behind the throne is a known pattern, but a foreigner is an outsider |
| **Ethiopia** | 4 | 2 | 2 | **3** | A strong king (the Zagwe dynasty) who funds big building projects, but **the Church and the nobility guard power**, and a new dynasty takes over in 1270 |
| **Central Chile** | 3 | 2 | 4 | **3** | The Mapuche have **no central ruler to win**; war leaders are chosen only for a war. Respect is easy to earn, but it can't become state power |
| **Japan** | 2 | 1 | 1 | **2** | **Hōjō regents rule through the shogun, who rules in the emperor's name.** Status is by birth and loyalty is to one's lord. A foreigner can at most be a valued retainer |
| **SE Australia** | 1 | 1 | **7** | **2** | No states or rulers to join. With his own settlers, he'd be a founder on nearly empty land (7) |

**Pattern:** **arriving with followers beats arriving alone almost everywhere**, because he founds something instead of climbing someone else's ladder. Old crowded kingdoms (Britain, Japan, Ethiopia) are the hardest to rule even with followers, because his people would be outsiders inside someone else's realm.
