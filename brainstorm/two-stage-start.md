## Short answer
**Two stages solve the biggest problem with the hidden homeland.** The places that are good to *build* in (hidden and rich) are hard places for a lone stranger to *rise* in. The places that are easy to rise in (rich courts that hire foreign experts) are bad places to build, because they are crowded with powerful rivals.

So the hero should:
1. **Stage 1, the climb:** start at a rich court as an *expert* and gather money, ships, skilled people and a reputation.
2. **Stage 2, the founding:** lead his own people to the hidden homeland. There he doesn't climb past anyone. **He arrives as the founder.**

Fibonacci and the Mongol refugees both fit this plan: **Fibonacci belongs to stage 1, and the refugees are the people he leads in stage 2.**

## How hard is it to rise? (1230s)
1–5, where **5 = easiest**. Being an *adviser* and being the *ruler* are very different.

| Place | Adviser, arriving alone | Ruler, arriving alone | Ruler, arriving with 2,000 followers | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Frederick II's court** (Sicily / southern Italy) | **5** | 1 | 1 | He collected foreign scholars (Michael Scot, Arab and Jewish translators) and tested people with math. But Europe has too many kings to ever rule there |
| **Pisa / Genoa / Venice** | 3 | 1 | 1 | Merchant republics. Money buys a seat, but noble families run them |
| **Mongol Empire** | 4 | 1 | 1 | It hires foreign experts, but he'd serve the empire everyone fears |
| **Mamluk / Ayyubid Egypt** | 3 | 2 | 2 | Merit counts: slave-soldiers could become sultan. Foreign doctors were honored |
| **Hungary in 1241** (after the Mongol attack) | 3 | 2 | 3 | Chaos: the king fled to the coast, and the land was emptied. A warlord's opening |
| **Japan** | 2 | 1 | 1 | Rigid loyalty to lords. (William Adams, an Englishman, did become a samurai, but in 1600) |
| **Mapungubwe** (southern Africa) | 2 | 2 | 3 | Language barrier, no shared customs. A small elite, a big tech gap. Marriage is the likely route |
| **Swahili coast** (Kilwa, Sofala) | 3 | 2 | 3 | Trading cities that welcomed Arab and Persian merchants who married in |
| **North America** (Great Lakes / Ohio) | 3 | 3 | **5** | Small chiefdoms and the biggest tech gap. With his own people he founds a state and doesn't need to take one over |
| **Empty land** (Madeira, Azores, New Zealand) | — | — | **5** | No one to rise past. Small or very far away |

**The pattern:** a stranger rises fastest as an *adviser* in a big, rich, open court. He becomes a *ruler* most easily by **bringing his own people** to a place with no strong state.

## Why the climb comes first
What stage 1 gives him that he can't get in a hidden land:
- **Money:** to buy ships, tools, seed, animals and books.
- **Skilled people:** smiths, glassmakers, shipwrights, masons, millers and doctors. These are the hardest things to make from scratch.
- **Ships and sailors:** the Mediterranean has the best shipyards outside China.
- **Proof and practice:** he tests his inventions where workshops already exist, *before* betting everything on a new land.
- **Followers who owe him:** healed families, trained students, rescued refugees.
- **A future trade partner:** his old contacts back home can buy his goods later without knowing where they come from. That partner is the "one gate."

What it costs: **10–20 years**, and the risk that a powerful patron won't let him leave.

## Who follows him: the Mongol refugees
The Mongol attack (1237–1242) creates exactly the people a founder needs: homeless, skilled, desperate and grateful.
- **Cumans (Kipchaks), 1239:** Khan Köten led about **40,000 families** into Hungary to escape the Mongols. Hungarian nobles murdered him in 1241, and the Cumans left in anger. They were a whole people with horses, cattle and warriors, and suddenly had nowhere to go.
- **Hungarians, 1241–42:** after the defeat at Mohi, a large share of the population died or fled. King Béla IV fled to Trogir on the Adriatic coast, close to Italy by sea.
- **Rus' (Kiev fell in 1240), Poles and Moravians**, plus earlier **Persians fleeing Khwarazm (1219–21)**, many of them master craftsmen.

The Mongols are a perfect *deadline* for stage 1 and a perfect *recruiting event* for stage 2. Later waves of immigrants arrive the same way (famines, the Black Death in 1347), which fits Tim's "maximum immigration."

**How many fit on ships?** A large medieval ship carries about 100–200 people. In 1202 Venice agreed to build a fleet for **33,500 men and 4,500 horses**. So a few thousand settlers with animals is realistic if he's rich.

### How plan A would change the homeland comparison
Before, I ranked North America third because of its **slow start**: no iron, no horses or oxen, no wheat, few people. **Stage 1 fixes all of these.** The refugee fleet *brings* the horses, cattle, wheat, iron-workers and people. What's left is the land with the best resources on Earth: Pittsburgh coal and iron, Lake Superior copper, oil, the Mississippi river network and the best farmland in the world. It stays hidden for 250+ years, and the interior stays safe even longer.

New problems it creates:
- **Disease runs the other way.** The settlers carry Old World diseases that could devastate Native peoples. The hero knows this and can **vaccinate with cowpox from the cattle he brings**, which could make saving the Americas from the epidemics part of the story. Native peoples are also the biggest immigration pool.
- **A long supply line.** There's no going back for parts. Everything critical comes in the first fleet.
- **A small start.** A few thousand people, so population growth and immigration matter even more.

### Why southern Africa is still in the running
Plan B or C still works well. Mapungubwe *already* has iron, cattle (cowpox) and a gold trade route, so less has to be carried. But he arrives into someone else's kingdom, so he still has to rise through it (marriage, healing, alliance) instead of simply founding.

## Fibonacci's role
- **Pisa** is a strong sea power, has Fibonacci, and trades with North Africa. **Frederick II** is the patron who tests people with math puzzles (Fibonacci met him around 1225).
- Possible scenes: the hero solves the court's problems *alongside* Fibonacci; he hands Fibonacci decimal fractions or algebra notation centuries early; Fibonacci becomes his first student, or his rival, or the friend who stays behind.
- Frederick could be **the one person told the truth** (Tim's single-prediction exception). He is curious, sceptical and loves experiments, so he'd want proof.
- Frederick's court is the **climb**, not the home: Europe is too crowded with rival powers to build the hidden state there.
