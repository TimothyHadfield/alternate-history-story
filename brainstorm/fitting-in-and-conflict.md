# Fitting in first, and keeping the peace

*For comparison across the six variations. History is from memory unless marked. Ratings are Claude's judgment.*

Tim (2026-10-28): "how language and culture matters if you're immediately joining into the group of people that you're with. A 2026 american will need to learn to live like a native american if they're going to start there before they're able to gain influence ... I think it's also important to think about conflicts that might happen due to predjudices, irrational beliefs, etc. For example if europeans think they have a god-right to the land or something I don't want them killing native americans ... just consider how movement and whatnot might cause limitations in our plan."

## Part 1: he has to live as them before he can lead them
**Influence comes after belonging.** In almost every society of 1230, a man with **no kin, no lord and no language is nobody**. People will ignore him, enslave him or kill him. Before anyone listens to his ideas, he has to survive, eat their food, do their work and talk.

### What "fitting in" costs him
| Need | What it means | Realistic time |
|---|---|---|
| **Language** | Immersion with no dictionary or teacher. He can write the sounds down in his own phonetic spelling, which speeds things up | **Basic talk in 6–12 months; fluent in 2–3 years** (adult immersion; reasoned) |
| **A place in the social web** | Every society has a way to absorb a stranger: **adoption into a family or clan, becoming a lord's client or servant, or marriage** | Months to years |
| **Daily life** | Their food (and stomach bugs), clothes, work, sleep, cleanliness, rules for men and women, gift obligations | 1 year to feel normal |
| **His body** | He has **no immunity to local diseases** (malaria in Africa and Java, local dysentery everywhere). He may carry colds and flu that are new to the Americas | First 1–2 years are the most dangerous |
| **Not looking dangerous** | A stranger with odd knowledge can be taken for a spirit, a spy or a **sorcerer**. When misfortune strikes, the outsider is the usual suspect | Ongoing; matters most in the first years |

**So in every variation, roughly years 0–2 go to fitting in, and real influence starts around year 2–5.** The plans already have small early proofs (healing, a well, bookkeeping). This makes clear that those proofs come **from inside the community, as one of them**, not as an outsider handing out gifts.

### The good news: most societies have a door for strangers
- **Adoption:** many Native nations later adopted outsiders fully into clans (this is recorded for the Iroquoians in the 1600s). An adopted person *is* family.
- **Clientship:** in southern Africa a stranger can become a big man's dependent, herding his cattle, and rise through loyalty.
- **Marriage:** the most common route in almost every society.
- **Skills:** courts in Europe, Java and the Islamic world hired foreign experts all the time. Frederick II's court was full of them.

## Part 2: conflict when groups move
Whenever people move (refugees, settlers, workers, neighbors joining), the danger is not the hero. It's **the newcomers and the locals distrusting each other**. Prejudice, religious certainty, land, women, cattle, revenge and disease are the usual sparks.

### A real warning from the exact period: the Cumans in Hungary
In **1239**, about 40,000 Cumans fleeing the Mongols were let into Hungary by King Béla IV. They were pagan herders. Their animals trampled Hungarian fields, and Hungarians accused them of rape and theft. In **March 1241**, with the Mongols invading, **a Hungarian mob murdered the Cuman khan Köten**. The Cumans left, burning their way south, **just when Hungary needed them most**.

**That's the same refugee group variation ① plans to gather.** Without strong rules, the fleet itself could fall into this.

### The "god-given right to the land" idea is younger than 1240
- The papal bulls that gave Christian kings a right to seize non-Christian lands (*Dum Diversis* 1452, *Romanus Pontifex* 1455, *Inter caetera* 1493) **came 200+ years later.**
- Around 1250, **Pope Innocent IV wrote the opposite**: non-Christians can lawfully own land and have their own rulers.
- **But crusading was very much alive:** the Teutonic Knights were conquering the pagan Prussians in the 1230s, and Sweden's own crusade to Finland happened around 1249.
- **So in 1240 a settler fleet doesn't automatically believe it owns America.** What it believes depends heavily on **who leads it and what rules it signs.** The hero can set the founding idea instead of inheriting the 1600s one.

### Why it would be different from the 1600s, and why it still could go wrong
| Then (1600s) | Here (1240s, with the hero) |
|---|---|
| Smallpox killed most Native people before the settlers even arrived, so the land looked "empty" | **Cowpox vaccine from day one**: Native nations stay strong partners |
| Settlers came with guns; Native people had none | Settlers have steel and horses, but are few, far from home and dependent on local help |
| Kings and companies wanted land and profit | **The founder writes the rules** before anyone lands |
| A steady flood of settlers for 300 years | A **few thousand** people on one fleet. Growth depends on getting along |

**What could still go wrong:**
- **Zealous priests** trying to convert by force.
- **Young men without wives** clashing over women.
- **Settlers' pigs and cattle eating Native crops**, which caused many real colonial wars.
- **Revenge cycles** after one killing.
- **Settlers' children 50 years on** forgetting the founding agreement.

## Part 3: safeguards that could fit any variation (options, not decisions)
1. **Learn first, lead later:** the hero lives as one of them for the first 1–2 years.
2. **A door in, not a door kicked open:** adoption, clientship or marriage, through the local leaders.
3. **A charter before the first step on land:**
   - **one law for everyone**, so killing a local is murder, the same as killing a settler
   - **land only by agreement or purchase**
   - **no forced conversion**
4. **Pick settlers carefully:** families and craftsmen rather than lone armed men. Leave out zealots and anyone with a record of violence. Keep the first waves small.
5. **Fence the animals:** livestock damage was a top cause of colonial wars.
6. **Translators from both sides:** a school where children from both groups grow up bilingual.
7. **Mixed councils and marriages:** the newcomers and locals share decisions.
8. **Disease plan:** quarantine on arrival, vaccinate everyone, and the hero's own isolation in the Americas.
9. **Shared wins:** common projects like dams, mills and storehouses, where both sides gain visibly.
10. **Pace the arrivals** to what the community can absorb. A rough rule (reasoned, not measured): **newcomers stay a minority in each settlement, and each wave settles before the next arrives.**

### What this changes in each variation
- **① North America:** the riskiest for violence. The plus: the hero leads a fleet he can shape **before** it lands. The charter, careful settler choice and **vaccinating the neighbors as a gift** are the core of this variation, not extras. The fleet's own Hungarian–Cuman feud has to be solved first. If he started in America alone instead, it becomes a "live as them for years" story, likely through **adoption into a clan**.
- **② Southern Africa:** the hardest start for him personally (alone, tonal language, malaria). The danger is **suspicion**: every well and cure has to come *through the king*, so the credit (and any blame) stays inside the system.
- **③ Britain:** he's a foreigner in a country that was growing angry at foreigners. A **local-born partner or front man** for the company, and English workers sharing in it, lower the risk.
- **④ Java:** the gentlest start. The risk is mostly court politics, not prejudice.
- **⑤ Andes:** a slow, quiet start. **His three-week quarantine** on arrival is essential. Unity comes from **joining voluntarily** (storehouse insurance), never from moving people by force.
- **⑥ Sweden:** the easiest language, but his patron leads a crusade into Finland within ten years. This variation has to decide **what he does about the Tavastia campaign**: steer it toward trade and treaties, or keep out of it.

### Movement limits worth adding to every plan
- **How fast newcomers can be absorbed** is a real cap on the "maximum immigration" idea.
- **Ships and food:** each fleet carries only so many people and animals, and **the first winter's food** limits how many can come.
- **Mixed groups need translators**, so the language school comes before the big waves.
- **The second generation** decides whether peace holds, so the plans need **schools that raise children of both groups together**.
