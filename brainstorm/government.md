# Government — how to speedrun growth

Tim (2026-10-27): "explore types of governments that would be best to speedrun growth and development both in population and technology and every other metric while I'm in charge. I'd say complete power to me would be best because I know best ... I also suspect some sort of maybe socialism could be good to provide people with food, healthcare, education, etc. ... I imagine essentially everyone would work for the government and because the things we're producing are so significant we could spend the excess money on research and exploration."

## Short answer
- **Complete power: yes at the start, no at the end.** It's the fastest way to launch, but a one-man system has two fatal limits: **one person can't see or decide everything**, and **he dies**.
- **Free food, health and schooling: yes, strongly.** It's the best investment available: cheap, and it's what makes population and skill explode.
- **Everyone works for the government: only for the big stuff.** History is clear that the state is great at building mines, railways and schools, and bad at running every farm and workshop.
- **What history says works best for catch-up:** a **"developmental state"**: a strong leader and an elite civil service run infrastructure, health, education and research, while people farm, craft and trade for themselves under rules the state sets. Meiji Japan, Prussia, South Korea and Singapore all did this.

## What history teaches about total control
**Where it shines:** catching up to a known target. The Soviet Union went from farming country to the world's #2 industrial power in ~30 years (1928–1960). Our hero has the *ultimate* known target: he knows exactly what works.

**Where it breaks:**
1. **The knowledge problem.** He knows *what* to build, but not which village is short of seed, which mine flooded, or which smith is brilliant. Central planners drown in detail. Markets and local managers solve this automatically.
2. **Bad news stops traveling up.** When disagreeing with the ruler is dangerous, officials report fake numbers. China's Great Leap Forward (1958–62) and the Soviet famines killed millions because officials reported the harvests the leader wanted.
3. **Incentives.** When everyone is paid the same no matter what, effort drops. Collective farms were famously less productive than private plots.
4. **No new ideas from below.** Command systems copy well but invent poorly. The hero's knowledge runs out eventually (he doesn't remember everything) and then the society needs its own inventors.
5. **Succession.** Every great one-man state faces "what happens after?" Alexander's empire split the day he died; Peter the Great's reforms stalled under weak heirs.

**The hero's edge:** he knows the answers, so he avoids the *big* planning mistakes (no Lysenko farming nonsense, no backyard furnaces). But problems 1, 2, 3 and 5 still apply to him.

## The "socialism" part: what to provide for everyone
These pay for themselves many times over:
- **Food security:** state granaries (store grain against the 1257 and 1315 famines), rationing in bad years. Egypt's pharaohs and the Inca did this.
- **Health:** clean water, latrines, midwives, vaccination, oral rehydration. Cheap and they double child survival.
- **Education:** free schools for every child: reading, math, hygiene, then trades and science. This is the single most important long-term investment; Prussia and Japan built their rise on it.
- **Pensions for the old and wounded** (loyalty, and people stop needing 8 children for old-age security).

## Who should work for the state, and who shouldn't
| State runs it | People run it (under state rules) |
|---|---|
| Mines, arsenals, weapons, gunpowder | Farms (own land, keep what they grow above a tax) |
| Roads, canals, ports, railways | Workshops, crafts, shops |
| Schools, hospitals, universities | Small trade and markets |
| Research labs, exploration, the mint | Inventions (rewarded with patents and prizes) |
| Army and navy | |

**Tools that fit a 1230s society:**
- **A labor tax / national service:** every adult gives a few weeks or years to the state (the Inca **mit'a** built 40,000 km of roads this way). Builds big projects and trains people at the same time.
- **Prizes for inventors:** Britain's Longitude Prize (1714) got the marine chronometer built.
- **State-owned "model factories"** that private copycats then spread (Japan did this in the 1870s: build it, prove it, sell it off).
- **Competition between teams** (two rival steel works, two rival schools) to keep the speed up.

**Fun fit:** the **Inca Empire** was nearly Tim's model: no money, a labor tax instead of a money tax, state storehouses full of food and cloth, everyone fed. It worked well for a while. It also collapsed instantly when its leader was captured. (Relevant if the story ends up in the Andes.)

## How fast can population grow?
- Medieval Europe grew ~0.1–0.5% a year. With food, health and peace, **2–3% a year** is possible (Kenya and others reached 3–4% in the 1900s).
- At 3% a year, the population **doubles every ~23 years**. 100,000 people → ~1.9 million in 100 years.
- **Immigration is the cheat code:** take in refugees and skilled people (fleeing Mongols, scholars from fallen Baghdad, persecuted craftsmen). The USA grew 3% a year in the 1800s largely this way.
- **The catch:** people take ~20 years to become workers. More children means more mouths to feed now and more workers later.

## Suggested path: three phases
| Phase | Years | Government | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Founder** | 0–15 | Hero has near-total power; small trusted inner circle | Speed, a small society, he's the only one who knows the plan |
| **2. Developmental state** | 15–50 | Hero still leads; a trained, merit-based civil service (exams, like China) runs ministries; markets for farms and crafts; state runs industry, schools, research | He can't manage a million people alone; incentives and local knowledge kick in |
| **3. Built to outlast him** | 50+ | Written laws that bind even rulers, councils, a chosen successor system, independent courts and record-keeping | He will die. The system has to survive a mediocre or bad successor |

## Feedback channels (so his power doesn't blind him)
- **Statistics everywhere:** harvests, births, deaths, output, independently counted by two separate offices.
- **Safe ways to report bad news:** an inspector corps that answers only to him; rewards for reporting problems, not hiding them.
- **Try it small first:** test every big reform in a few villages before rolling it out (China's "special economic zones" approach).
